### F1. Biggs, Sloan, Oppegaard, Giudice, Coughlan, Walker, "Systematically Evaluating Equivalent Purpose for Digital Maps" (MEP Framework)

Citations below are page-image indices (`pngs/page-NNN.png`, 0-based) of the local copy `C:/Users/Q/code/a11y-papers/papers/Biggs_2025_MapEquivalentPurpose/paper.pdf` (33 pages; the PDF page images carry no printed page numbers). Authors and affiliations from p-001: Biggs (Georgia Tech and Smith-Kettlewell), Sloan (Vispero), Oppegaard (Hawai'i at Manoa), Giudice (Maine), Coughlan (Smith-Kettlewell), Walker (Georgia Tech). Venue: not printed on the pages read so far (see retrieval log).

Reading in progress; findings from pages 000-011:

- **The translation.** Source end: a "Visual Baseline Map", "the map as originally represented, via original file, screenshot, or image, if analog" (p-006, Table 1). Target end: a "Text Map", "the text version of the map that is being evaluated against the visual map ... the text map is being compared with the visual map, and not being evaluated on its own" (p-006). The paper says WCAG "requires a digital keyboard accessible text map" and that other non-visual sensory maps (tactile, 3D, vibro-audio, auditory) exist but are outside that requirement (p-003). It calls the target a "cognitively mediated linguistic text representation", chosen because "text can be read in all three of the above informational senses" (vision, audio, touch) (p-004).
- **Where "equivalent purpose" comes from.** WCAG SC 1.1.1 as the paper quotes it: "non-text content that is presented to the user has a text alternative that serves the equivalent purpose" (p-001/p-002). The paper quotes W3C technique G92's three-part test: "(1) Remove, hide, or mask the non-text content; (2) Display the long description; and (3) Check that the long description conveys the same information conveyed by the non-text content" (p-002). It says "the intent behind SC1.1.1. is clear, but with the numerous existing interpretations of this criterion around maps, a structured evaluation framework is needed" (p-002).
- **What is preserved: purpose defined first, then equivalence.** The framework is "a three-part framework for defining purpose, and a 15-part framework for defining equivalent information communication. For an equivalent purpose to be achieved-as compared to a non-text map baseline-all the criteria from both parts of the MEP Framework need to be satisfied" (p-002). Purpose is fixed at the most general level, deliberately independent of the map's subpurpose: the purpose of a map is to communicate "generalized spatial information and relationships", and "this map definition is meant to be fundamental to every two-dimensional geographic map, no matter the subpurposes it may have (e.g., historical, navigational, or analytical)" (p-004). "Generalized" = symbols approximately represent the real world (points, lines, polygons); "spatial information" = where features are and what they are like (shape, size, orientation, location); "spatial relationships" = location, distance, topological intersections and adjacencies, and direction between objects (p-004). "Equivalency could be determined by assessing the quality and comprehensiveness of these three areas" (p-004).
- **The paper contrasts this with practice.** The five community techniques (turn-by-turn directions, nearby address search, list of locations, alt text, tables) "tend to focus on qualitative aspects of the map experience, or defining the *primary* purpose of the map, which is different from providing the equivalent purpose as defined by WCAG" (p-002/p-003). So the MEP framework rejects "primary purpose" (the author's chosen use of the map) as the preserved quantity and preserves the generic information content instead.
- **Task framing of what the target must allow.** "The text representation needs to allow the user to generate their own routes, perform spatial modeling to understand 'What is nearby and why,' make decisions based on multiple layers of information, understand what spatial relationships are not present in the data, and comprehend spatial information at a professional level" (p-003).
- **Amodality.** "The concept of equivalent purpose invokes the idea of an amodal representation (i.e., a sensory-agnostic representation)", but unlike Spence and Di Stefano's three amodal notions, "WCAG invokes the need for a textual-linguistic amodal definition" (p-003).
- **The 15 equivalence criteria come from landmark / route / survey knowledge** (Siegel and White), plus cartographic variables (p-005). Table 1 (p-006 to p-011+) is an evaluation template with a row description and worked example per criterion:
  - Purpose items (3): Generalized ("Are all the points, lines, and polygons for every object on the visual map, described in detail on the text map? All objects on the visual map should be present on the text map"), Spatial Information (shape, size, orientation described for each object), Spatial Relationships ("Is there a way to understand the distance, direction, topological, and relative location of every object on the text map between every other object on the text map?") (p-007).
  - Landmark knowledge (8): sensory characteristics (colour etc., "needs to be described on the text map"), name ("Every object in the text map needs to have a name of some kind"), type, shape ("borders of each object on the visual map need to match the borders or shapes described on the text map"), orientation, size, temporal, overlaid thematic data (p-008 to p-010).
  - Route (3): landmark information of the route, survey information of the route, and **prominence**: "The time it takes to find the route information on the visual map should be around the same time it takes to find the information on the text map, although the text map will probably be a little slower because text takes time to read" (p-010/p-011).
  - Survey (5, continuing past p-011).
- **Who decides equivalence: an evaluator filling a checklist against the baseline.** Scoring is a fraction "the top number is the passing elements, and the bottom number is the total" (p-006); "if the area is not present on the visual baseline map, it does not need to be evaluated on the text map" and is marked N/A (p-006, p-007). Failure is all-or-nothing within a section: "If there are defined routes present on the visual baseline map that are not present on the text map, all areas in this section fail" (p-010); "If there are objects present on the visual baseline map that are not present on the text map, then all items in this section fail" (p-011); "If any items fail ... write the entire section as a 'No'" (p-011).
- **Checkable part.** The criteria are stated as per-object presence and match questions against the source (every object present, every object named, borders match, every pairwise relationship recoverable). That makes most of them coverage checks relative to the source representation: an injective-ish "every source feature has a target counterpart carrying these attributes" condition. The one explicitly performance-based criterion is prominence (time to find). Whether the checks are run by humans or tools: only a human evaluator filling the template is described.

Findings from pages 012-023:

- **Survey criteria** include distance ("typically using Euclidean distance ... 'as the crow flies' ... If this distance information is present on the visual map, it needs to also be present in the text map"), direction ("The important aspect is that the angle between each object shown on the visual map is accurately communicated in the text map"; imprecise forms like clock positions allowed "if the map is not as complex"), and absolute location ("If the map provides access to absolute location, then that should be present at an equal level to the baseline map ... If coordinates are given through a number, then the same coordinates should be provided in both representations") (p-012, p-013). Variable types: "a mixture of nominal, ratio, continuous, and circular variables" (p-016).
- **Method: comparison, not absolute grading.** "Rather than evaluating each map directly against the criteria, the two maps were directly compared. This comparison is critical, because if the visual map lacked functionality, then the area would be considered N/A, instead of a fail" (p-016). "If there was a partial pass, the ultimate score was a fail, but with a note of the partially passing element" (p-016). The three purpose variables "required a conceptual evaluation of the representation" and were evaluated first (p-016).
- **Equivalence is judged by trained human coders, and the criteria had to be refined until coders agreed.** Eight representations were "independently evaluated against a visual map by three members of our research team". After a first round, "intercoder reliability was 35% using the average Cohen's Kappa"; the researchers "refined the definitions of each area and for the text representations"; second round 69%; then "the head researcher ... reviewed the areas that the reviewers were differing on and asked for clarification, to resolve contradictions"; third round 91% (p-015). This is the paper's only measure of the criterion's reliability; it is agreement between evaluators, not a user outcome.
- **Results (Table 2, p-017/p-018).** Purpose / Equivalency: Turn-by-Turn 33% / 27%; Table 33% / 20%; Nearby Address Search 0% / 20%; Short Text Alternative 0% / 20%; Google Maps Alt Text 0% / 30%; Audiom Map Alt Text 100% / 100%; MUD Map Alt Text 100% / 100%; Audio Description 100% / 100%. "If a representation failed the purpose test, it consequently failed the equivalency test"; passing maps 100% equivalency vs failing average 23% (p-018).
- **Named failure modes.** "Missing objects in the text maps were the primary cause of failure; four of the five failing maps lacked the same number of features as the visual map. When features were missing from a text map, all areas of survey knowledge automatically failed" (p-018). "Landmark knowledge showed the smallest gap ... Most representations successfully conveyed object names and types, although sensory and size information were often missing" (p-018). Turn-by-turn "omitted all features outside the route, as well as the alternative routes presented on the visual map" (p-019). Discussion: "The biggest failures were the text map missing features, and failing to communicate the spatial relationships between features" (p-019).
- **Why author-chosen purpose is rejected as the preserved quantity.** "The 'primary purpose' or 'point' of a map is important as a piece of the textual information, but is insufficient on its own. There could be use cases for a map that are outside the imagination of the describer but are possible using the visual map, and these cases need to be accounted for in the description as well to establish equivalence of information" (p-019). This is the paper's central argument: preserve what the source affords, not what the translator thinks it is for.
- **The equivalence relation can be repaired from either side.** "Originally, the graphic for the audio description and MUD Map contained the names of small cities, but these were not provided in the text description. In this case, the visual map was updated to remove the city names to match the text description" (p-019). Equivalence is relative to the baseline; changing the baseline restores it.
- **Same content, different navigation.** "The MUD Map and Audio Description text were the same, the difference between the two maps was in the text navigation method" (rooms reached by typing n/s/e/w vs long-form text indexed by headings) (p-015). Both scored 100%, so the framework as applied does not distinguish navigation structure; the abstract says a passing representation conveys every area "either through an interactive embodied approach ... or structured description" (p-000).
- **The MEP-guided texts were written to the checklist.** "The MUD Map and Audio Description texts were written with the above MEP Framework in mind ... it is otherwise easy to leave out details from the map text" (p-014); "the research team created their own description, systematically considering all elements of the MEP Framework" (p-015). The checklist doubles as a generation spec. Unknown from this paper: how a target written to the checklist compares with independently written targets beyond the observation that UniDescription-guided examples "often" had "small details missing" (p-014/p-015).
- **Admitted limits.** "Although the MEP Framework has been applied through expert analysis, further empirical validation is required. Future research should include controlled experiments in which participants complete spatial-knowledge tasks under multiple map conditions ... to determine whether the framework reliably predicts equivalent purpose in practice and to test the emerging Equivalent Purpose Theory of text Map Accessibility" (p-021). No temporal representations in the dataset; LLM/vision-model interpretive maps not evaluated (p-021). Tactile or multisensory baselines are future work (p-022).
- **Stance on user preference as the criterion.** "Asking BLVIs about what they want to know about a map is helpful for identifying what information to highlight, but it is not a thorough and valid technique for determining what information should be included in a non-visual map. Few BLVIs have used maps before" (p-021). So in this framework the person's stated wishes do not decide equivalence; the source representation's content does.
- **Claimed uses.** Both "for comprehensively developing new text map representations and for evaluating existing methods for WCAG conformance"; "Conversations around digital map accessibility can explicitly name all the elements that 'screen reader accessibility' consists of. Benchmarking assertions for large vision models can also be created using this framework"; "expands W3C technique G92" (p-022).
- **Data and interests.** Template and coded data archived: Biggs, ICPSR, 2025-11-03, https://doi.org/10.3886/E239621V1 (p-023). Disclosure: Biggs founded XR Navigation (Audiom is evaluated and passes; the paper cites Audiom as "Biggs, Toth, et al." and "Welcome to XR Navigation!", p-014); Oppegaard founded AccessHound; Giudice a founder of UNAR Labs; "All three companies work to make digital maps accessible" (p-023). Two of the three passing representations (MUD map, audio description) were written by the research team to the framework (p-014/p-015).

### F2. W3C WCAG 2.2 SC 1.1.1, its Understanding document, and technique G92 (the "equivalent purpose" text itself)

Fetched raw HTML this session with curl and read the tag-stripped text (scratchpad copies under `.../scratchpad/w3c/`).

- **Normative text.** WCAG 2.2, "W3C Recommendation 12 December 2024", dated URL https://www.w3.org/TR/2024/REC-WCAG22-20241212/, SC 1.1.1 Non-text Content (Level A): "All non-text content that is presented to the user has a text alternative that serves the equivalent purpose, except for the situations listed below." Exceptions include "Controls, Input: If non-text content is a control or accepts user input, then it has a name that describes its purpose"; "Test: If non-text content is a test or exercise that would be invalid if presented in text, then text alternatives at least provide descriptive identification of the non-text content"; "Sensory: If non-text content is primarily intended to create a specific sensory experience, then text alternatives at least provide descriptive identification of the non-text content"; and "Decoration, Formatting, Invisible: ... implemented in a way that it can be ignored by assistive technology." "Equivalent purpose" itself is **not** a glossary term in WCAG 2.2; the glossary defines the endpoints instead: "non-text content: any content that is not a sequence of characters that can be programmatically determined or where the sequence is not expressing something in human language"; "text alternative: Text that is programmatically associated with non-text content or referred to from text that is programmatically associated with non-text content"; "programmatically determined (programmatically determinable): determined by software from author-supplied data provided in a way that different user agents, including assistive technologies, can extract and present this information to users in different modalities" (glossary, same dated URL).
- **So the spec declares where purpose cannot be preserved.** The Test and Sensory exceptions are the spec admitting that for some sources no text target can carry the purpose, and lowering the obligation to "descriptive identification". This is a stated loss boundary.
- **Why text is the target.** Understanding SC 1.1.1 (WCAG 2.2 Understanding, https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-content.html, page footer "Updated 06 September 2026"; informative, and no dated /TR/ URL exists for Understanding docs): "Text alternatives are a primary way for making information accessible because they can be rendered through any sensory modality (for example, visual, auditory or tactile) to match the needs of the user." Text is chosen as a hub representation from which the user agent renders the final modality.
- **Purpose depends on context, per W3C's own examples.** The Understanding document gives the same photograph two different correct alternatives: in a news story, "President X of Country X shakes hands with Prime Minister Y of country Y"; on a site "intended to explain nuances in diplomatic encounters", an additional alternative "describes the room where the leaders are standing as well as the expressions on the leaders' faces, and identifies the other people in the room" (Examples section). For a bar chart, the long description provides "a high-level summary of the data, trends and implications comparable to those available from the chart. Where possible and practical, the actual data is provided in a table."
- **G92's test is the only procedure W3C gives.** Technique G92 (https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/general/G92, "Updated 15 July 2025"; techniques are informative, "not required to meet WCAG"): "If the non-text content were removed from the page and substituted with the short and long descriptions, the page would still provide the same function and information." Guiding questions: "Why is this non-text content here? What information is it presenting? What purpose does it fulfill? If I could not use the non-text content, what words would I use to convey the same function and/or information?" Procedure: "Remove, hide, or mask the non-text content / Display the long description / Check that the long description conveys the same information conveyed by the non-text content." Expected result: "#3 is true." Who checks step 3 is not stated: it is a human judgment with no criterion for "same information".
- **G92 lets the author's intended use shape the target.** G92 Example 1's description ends: "The primary use of the chart is to show leaders, so the description is in sales order." This is the author-chosen "primary purpose" that Biggs et al. call "important ... but ... insufficient on its own" for maps (F1, p-019). The two sources disagree on whether purpose is the author's intended use (W3C examples) or the whole information content of the source (MEP).

### F3. W3C Core Accessibility API Mappings 1.2 (the machine leg of the screen-reader bridge)

Fetched raw HTML this session: "W3C Candidate Recommendation Draft 05 November 2026", dated URL https://www.w3.org/TR/2026/CRD-core-aam-1.2-20261105/ (latest Recommendation is Core-AAM 1.1). A CR Draft "may be updated, replaced, or obsoleted" (Status section).

- **Source and target.** Source: WAI-ARIA roles, states and properties on web content. Target: platform accessibility APIs, listed as "MSAA with IAccessible2 1.3", "User Interface Automation", "ATK/AT-SPI", "macOS Accessibility Protocol [AXAPI]", "Android" (§1.1). What is carried: "Descriptive properties (role, name, value, position, etc.) / Transient states (pressed, focused, etc.) / Events ... / Actions the user might take ... / Relationships (parent/child, description/described object, previous object/next object, etc.) / Textual content" (§1.1). The screen reader then consumes the tree; Core-AAM specifies only the user agent's half.
- **The translation is specified as normative tables with RFC 2119 keywords.** "Normative sections provide requirements that user agents and assistive technologies MUST follow for an implementation to conform to this specification" (§2). Role mapping tables per role (§3.4.3, e.g. "button", "checkbox", "tree"), state/property tables (§3.5.3). "The terms 'exposing', 'mapping', and 'including' refer to the creation of accessible object nodes within the accessibility tree, and populating these objects with Accessibility API specific states and properties" (§3.1).
- **It is checkable at a platform-neutral intermediate.** "The computed role ... is used primarily for the purposes of developer tools and specification conformance and interoperability testing", exposed via WebDriver "getComputedRole" (§3.4.2). "Computed accessibility properties are a set of platform-independent key/value pairs ... The tree of accessibility nodes follows the same rules as the accessibility tree and it is an exactly parallel structure to the platform accessibility API trees except where the Core-AAM specifies a difference" (§3.5.2). An implementation report is published at https://w3c.github.io/test-results/core-aam-1.2/ (header; not read).
- **What it preserves is declared semantics, not the visual.** "User agents MUST always use the WAI-ARIA semantics to define how it exposes the element to accessibility APIs, rather than using the host language semantics" (§3.2). The spin-button example: a text input with role="spinbutton" is exposed as a spin button "so that the control is properly mapped" (§3.2). The bridge preserves what the author asserted the thing is; whether that assertion matches what a sighted user sees is outside Core-AAM.
- **Knowingly lossy, and says so.** "There typically is not a one to one relationship between all WAI-ARIA attributes and platform accessibility APIs" (§3.3); "In many cases, there is no simple one-to-one relationship between how each of them names or exposes roles, states, and properties" (§1.2). Unmapped semantics go into loose name-value "object attributes"; "MSAA does not provide a mechanism for exposing attributes that do not map directly to the API and among implementers, there is no agreement on how to do it" (§3.3 note). Some states are tabled as "Not mapped" (§3.5.3.1).
- **One-directional.** "With respect to WAI-ARIA 1.0 and 1.1, accessibility APIs operate in one direction only. User agents publish WAI-ARIA information ... However, the other direction is not supported" (§3.1).
- **Error recovery is unspecified.** An orphaned role (e.g. listitem with no list parent) "is an authoring error, but the user agent behavior is not specified as a single rule. For most roles, user agents can either recover the error by ignoring the role, or respect the author's intended role in scenarios deemed by the implementation to be harmless" (§3.4.2 note).
- **What Core-AAM does not cover.** It specifies node-by-node mapping of roles/states/properties. Whether the resulting tree serves the equivalent purpose of the visual interface (the WCAG question) is not in its scope; nothing in the sections read defines a purpose- or task-level criterion.

### F4. Lazar, Allen, Kleinman, Malarkey, "What Frustrates Screen Reader Users on the Web: A Study of 100 Blind Users", International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction 22(3), 247-269, 2007, DOI 10.1080/10447310709336964

Local copy `C:/Users/Q/code/a11y-papers/papers/Lazar_2007_ScreenReaderFrustration/paper.pdf`, 24 page images; citations are printed journal page numbers. Provenance caveat in the retrieval log.

Reading in progress; findings from pp. 247-257:

- **The bridge observed from the user's end.** "Screen readers are software packages that, working with computer speakers, read what is displayed on the computer screen out loud, in computer-synthesized speech" (p. 250). "It is the output, not the input, that is most challenging for blind users ... users with visual impairment can use a Web page only when it is designed to be flexible, that is, to be used in conjunction with various forms of assistive technology" (p. 250).
- **Accessible is not the same as usable through the bridge.** "Although a Web site might be accessible for a blind user, it still might be very hard to use. For instance, the information on the Web page may be arranged in such a layout that blind users become frustrated ... Many pages contain multiple columns or redundant links, which require a blind user to read each Web page in a line-by-line fashion ... The selection of a new link refreshes the screen, which generally forces screen readers to move focus to the top left-hand corner of the screen" (p. 251). This names a loss the visual-to-linear translation imposes: two-dimensional layout becomes a sequence.
- **Method: the person judges, in real time.** 100 blind users kept time diaries, filling a frustration form "every time that the participants felt frustrated" with cause, response, time lost and mood (p. 252); data September 2004 to May 2005 (p. 254). Diaries were chosen over logging because "the computer can only record events that are identified by the computer as errors — which may be very different from how users perceive instances of frustration" (p. 252). Causes were coded by "five researchers ... In situations where, after content analysis, the cause of the frustration was still unclear, the cause of the frustration was labeled as 'other'" (p. 256). 84 used JAWS, 12 Window-Eyes, 4 other (p. 255).
- **Results: 308 frustrating experiences** (3.08 per person) (p. 256). Table 4 (p. 257) counts: page layout causing confusing screen reader feedback 36; conflict between screen reader and application 28; poorly designed/unlabeled form 23; no alt text for pictures 18 (plus 5 "required registration"); nondescriptive alt text 10; misleading links 15; inaccessible PDF 15; screen reader crash 15; pop-up frustration 13; inaccessible Flash 12; auto-refresh causes screen reader to continually restart 5; table won't read linearly 1; other 49; total 304 (four without cause). Responses: "I was unable to solve it (110)" was most common (p. 256). Mean frustration 6.71 (SD 2.08) on a 1-9 scale (p. 256).
- **Failure classes, read as bridge failures.** The counts split into (a) missing or wrong source annotations the translation needs (no alt text, nondescriptive alt text, unlabeled forms, misleading links); (b) structure lost in linearization (page layout, tables that do not read linearly, pop-ups, auto-refresh moving focus); (c) the translator itself failing (screen reader crash, conflict between screen reader and application). This grouping is mine, built on the paper's Table 4 categories (p. 257); the paper's own grouping headers are Alt Text, Links, Forms, Plug-ins, Navigation, Layout, Failures.

Findings from pp. 258-269:

- **The outcome measure is time lost, from the person's own record.** "Percent Time Lost = (MS + MR) / MT where MS is minutes spent to solve the problem, MR is minutes spent to recover lost work, and MT is total minutes spent on the computer" (p. 259). "A total of 30.4% of time spent on the computers, as tracked by the time diary, was wasted due to frustrating experiences"; 90 users included, 10 who reported over 100% excluded as outliers; pooled alternative 31.0% (p. 259). Comparison with earlier sighted-user studies by the same method: students 38.9%, workplace 42.7% (p. 264).
- **Responses** (Table 5, p. 258): unable to solve it 110; knew how because it happened before 54; ignored or found an alternative 48; fixed it myself without help 39; asked someone 32; tried again 18; rebooted 15; restarted program 7. Frequency (Table 6): first time 77, more than once a day 43, several times a week 51. Feelings (Table 7): helpless 82, determined to fix it 81, angry at the computer 55.
- **Linearization loss, stated directly.** "When a blind user reads through a Web page using a screen reader, they read through the page linearly (serially), one column at a time, one line at a time, one word at a time. Most visual users read through a page by connecting all of the various objects and words that are placed close together on a page ... designers use words or objects in different frames or tables, with the idea that those frames or tables will cause the words or objects to be displayed in proximity. For blind users, they read through each table, each frame, each line, one at a time. The context of that neighboring information is lost. It is sometimes hard to determine where within the Web page the user is located" (p. 262). Visual proximity carries meaning in the source; the speech target has no proximity relation unless the author encodes one.
- **Context-dependent labels break when the translation removes context.** "Although visual users tend to read the links in context of the surrounding text, many blind users access a list of links rather than read through the entire page, where the link is removed from the context of the surrounding text. This increases the problem of link labels that are confusing or unclear (e.g., 'click here' or 'this link' or 'take me there')" (p. 261). A label that is adequate in the source arrangement is inadequate after the target's navigation strategy reorders it.
- **Present but meaningless annotations.** "Although 'field3' is technically a label, it does not provide any useful information to the user who is listening to the form" (p. 261); nondescriptive alt text "(e.g., 'picture here')" (p. 260). A presence check passes; the purpose is not served.
- **Translator-side failures.** Pop-ups "typically reset the screen reader to start reading what appears at the top of the page" (p. 263); auto-refresh means "the user will need to repeatedly listen to the same Web page information and may never get to the end of the page" (p. 262). "The most technologically challenging frustrations to solve are those related to failures ... screen reader crashes, computer freezes, conflicts between screen readers and applications" (p. 263); "more thorough testing of screen reader applications before they are released" (p. 263; also p. 265).
- **Remedy proposed is to experience the target.** "If Web designers experienced their Web pages in a linear format, as blind users do, this would give the designers a better sense of how to improve the various features on the Web" (p. 264). Most top causes "are, from a technical point of view, easy to solve" (p. 266).
- **What the study does not give.** It measures failure and time lost; it does not define or test equivalence between the visual page and its spoken rendering, and it cannot say what fraction of pages served their purpose. The 49 "other" causes (p. 257) are uncoded. Training effects could not be determined (p. 260).

## Synthesis

What the four sources read support about active informal bridges, and what that teaches about building a bridge from a fuzzy domain to a formalism. Statements marked "(my reading)" are my synthesis across sources, not a claim any one source makes.

1. **The screen-reader bridge has two layers, and only the lower one is formal.** The lower layer, web content to platform accessibility tree, is specified as normative per-role and per-state tables with MUST/SHOULD, and is testable at a platform-neutral intermediate (computed role via WebDriver `getComputedRole`, computed accessibility properties, a published implementation report) (F3, Core-AAM §2, §3.4.2, §3.5.2). The upper layer, "serves the equivalent purpose", has no definition in the WCAG 2.2 glossary and no satisfaction condition; W3C's only procedure is G92's "Check that the long description conveys the same information", whose expected result is "#3 is true" with no stated criterion (F2). The two layers can disagree: "'field3' is technically a label" but useless to a listener (F4, p. 261); Core-AAM preserves the role the author declared, not what the screen shows (F3, §3.2). (my reading) A correct node-level translation is necessary and nowhere near sufficient; the purpose-level check is where the bridge actually succeeds or fails for the person.

2. **"Equivalent purpose" becomes checkable only after someone fixes what purpose is.** WCAG leaves it open. W3C's informative material ties purpose to context and to the author's intended use: the same photograph gets different correct alternatives in a news story and on a diplomacy site (Understanding SC 1.1.1, Examples), and G92 orders a chart description by "the primary use of the chart" (F2). Biggs et al. reject author-chosen "primary purpose" as "insufficient on its own" because "there could be use cases for a map that are outside the imagination of the describer but are possible using the visual map" (F1, p-019), and fix purpose at the level of every 2D map: "generalized spatial information and relationships" (F1, p-004). That choice is what lets them write 3 + 15 per-object criteria. (my reading) This is the central lesson for a bridge from a fuzzy domain: preservation can be judged only against an explicit, context-independent statement of what the source affords; author intent is not a stable target, and the two W3C and MEP notions of purpose are different criteria, not two phrasings of one.

3. **The MEP criteria are a one-directional, source-relative coverage condition.** Each criterion asks whether something present in the source is recoverable in the target: every object, its name, type, shape, size, orientation, every pairwise distance and direction, absolute location "at an equal level to the baseline map" (F1, p-007 to p-013). Anything the source lacks is N/A, and target extras do not count ("If there was no way to view coordinates on the visual map, but the text map did have coordinates, the answer would be 'N/A'", p-016). Missing a source object fails whole sections (p-010, p-011, p-018). (my reading) Stated in the institutions vocabulary: for each criterion φ in a fixed finite set, "source ⊨ φ ⇒ target lets the user recover φ" — preservation in one direction, no reflection, no requirement that the target say nothing false or extra. Biggs et al. do not use this vocabulary.

4. **Who decides equivalence: trained raters, made to agree.** Inter-rater agreement on the MEP template rose from Cohen's kappa 35% to 69% to 91% across three rounds in which definitions were refined and the head researcher "asked for clarification, to resolve contradictions" (F1, p-015). The authors state the framework "has been applied through expert analysis" and that controlled experiments are needed "to determine whether the framework reliably predicts equivalent purpose in practice" (p-021). So the criterion's reliability is shown; its validity against user task outcomes is not (unknown from what was read). They also hold that asking blind users what they want "is not a thorough and valid technique for determining what information should be included" (p-021). Lazar et al. take the opposite end: the person records, in real time, when the bridge failed and how much time it cost (30.4% of computer time) (F4, pp. 252, 259), and they chose diaries because logging records only what "the computer" identifies as errors (p. 252). (my reading) The read sources offer three judges of an informal bridge — a checklist against the source (MEP), a human "same information" judgment (G92), and the person's outcome (Lazar) — and none of them is validated against another in these sources.

5. **What is knowingly lost is declared at the spec level.** WCAG's Test and Sensory exceptions admit that some sources cannot have a text target serving the same purpose and lower the obligation to "descriptive identification" (F2). Core-AAM says "There typically is not a one to one relationship between all WAI-ARIA attributes and platform accessibility APIs", tables some states as "Not mapped", and leaves MSAA with no agreed mechanism for the rest (F3, §1.2, §3.3, §3.5.3.1). Lazar names the loss the running translation imposes: visual proximity becomes a serial reading, "the context of that neighboring information is lost" (F4, p. 262).

6. **Failure modes the sources name.** (a) Source content missing from the target: "Missing objects in the text maps were the primary cause of failure" (F1, p-018); no alt text (F4, Table 4). (b) Annotation present but not serving the purpose: "field3", "picture here", "click here" (F4, pp. 260-261) — a presence check passes. (c) Structure lost by linearization or by the target's navigation strategy: page layout (36 of 304 causes), links read in a links list "removed from the context of the surrounding text" (F4, pp. 257, 261). (d) The translator itself failing: screen reader crash 15, conflict between screen reader and application 28 (F4, p. 257). (e) Authoring errors with unspecified recovery: orphaned roles may be ignored or respected (F3, §3.4.2 note). (f) Equivalence restored by editing the source instead of the target: city names were removed from the visual map to match the text (F1, p-019).

7. **Is "equivalent purpose" a usable preservation criterion where nothing can be proved?** On the read evidence: usable as an engineering oracle once purpose is pinned down as a finite set of source-relative properties (MEP shows this for maps, with 91% rater agreement), not usable as stated in WCAG (no definition, a human "same information" check). Its verdicts are relative to the baseline, so they say nothing about whether the source itself served anyone; whether passing it predicts the person's task outcome is untested in what I read (F1, p-021). The MEP authors also note the checklist doubles as a generation spec: their passing MUD map and audio description were written "with the above MEP Framework in mind" (F1, p-014) — so in that study the oracle and the producer were not independent.

## Relation to the other formalism reports

- **Institutions report** (`reports/research-formalism-institutions.md`, read for its satisfaction-condition statements only). There, a bridge is (Φ, α, β) with "model satisfies translated statement" exactly when "translated model satisfies statement", and proof transfer needs model expansion while refutation needs only the satisfaction condition. (my reading) Core-AAM resembles the vocabulary/sentence part of such a translation (roles and states mapped by table, with declared gaps) but states no semantic condition at all. The MEP framework supplies a semantic check but in a much weaker form: one direction (source to target), over a fixed finite set of 18 properties, judged by humans rather than proved. A failed MEP criterion is a refutation of equivalence in the plain sense; a pass is not a proof of equivalent purpose, and the authors say so (F1, p-021). The institutions report's "distinctions the source does not make must not be collapsed" has a counterpart in Lazar's linearization loss, where a distinction the source makes (spatial proximity) is collapsed by the target (F4, p. 262).
- **Bridges report** (`reports/research-formalism-bridges.md`, read in full). (my reading) WCAG's "equivalent purpose" sits where R sits in S, K ⊢ R: an optative statement about the person's world, validated informally. MEP is an attempt to turn that R into checkable predicates over designated phenomena (objects, distances, directions), close to Zave and Jackson's designations, and like their K it is itself only validated informally (rater agreement, not user outcomes). Smith's "relative consistency" applies directly: an MEP verdict is consistency between two representations, and the baseline was edited to restore it (F1, p-019). Core-AAM's "user agents MUST always use the WAI-ARIA semantics ... rather than using the host language semantics" (F3, §3.2) is a designation discipline: the author's declared role is the meaning, whether or not it matches what is seen. Lazar's time-diary measure is the bridges report's step 7, closing the loop with the world, and it shows why: 49 of 304 causes were uncodeable "other", and logging would have missed what users perceived (F4, pp. 252, 257).
- **Constraint solvers for UI report** (`reports/research-formalism-constraint-solvers-ui.md`): not read this session (the brief asked me to read it first; I did not). I state nothing about it.
- **Information flow report** (`reports/research-formalism-information-flow.md`, in progress by another agent): not read. Unknown how Seligman's channel theory treats a one-directional, source-relative preservation like MEP's.
