## Findings

### [M93] McCarthy, Notes on Formalizing Context

- **What a context is.** "The basic relation is ist(c, p). It asserts that the proposition p is true in the context c" (abstract, p.1). Main formulas have the form "c′ : ist(c, p)", "assertions that the proposition p is true in the context c, itself asserted in an outer context c′" (p.2, eq.1). "Contexts are abstract objects. We don't offer a definition" (p.2). Some contexts are "rich objects": "the context associated with a conversation is rich; we cannot list all the common assumptions of the participants ... On the other hand, the contexts associated with certain microtheories are poor and can be completely described" (p.2). In Remark 4, "context" is expected to be an "approximate theory": it "will appear in useful axioms and other sentences but will not have a definition involving 'if and only if'" (pp.11-12).
- **Terms vary too.** value(c, term): "the space in which terms take values may itself be context dependent", though "many applications will not require this generality" (p.3). Example: number of Holmes's wives is 0 in the Sherlock Holmes stories context and 1 in the U.S. legal history context (p.3). at(jmc, Stanford) means "regularly at" in c1 and "physical presence ... at a certain instant" in c2 (p.3).
- **Assumptions made explicit.** assuming(p, c) is "another context like c in which p is assumed" (p.4). "specializes(c1, c2) when c2 involves no more assumptions than c1 and every proposition meaningful in c1 is translatable into one meaningful in c2" (p.4). Entering a context is "analogous to assuming c in a system of natural deduction"; "a context is a generalization of a collection of assumptions, but there are important differences": contexts contain "linguistic assumptions as well as declarative", and "a context may correspond to an infinite and only partially known collection of assumptions" (p.5).
- **Lifting.** The first lifting relation: ist(specialize-time(t, c), at(jmc, Stanford)) ≡ ist(c, at-time(t, at(jmc, Stanford))) (p.4). The general inheritance rules are nonmonotonic, with an abnormality condition each way (p.4):
  - specializes(c1, c2) ∧ ¬ab1(p, c1, c2) ∧ ist(c1, p) ⊃ ist(c2, p)
  - specializes(c1, c2) ∧ ¬ab2(p, c1, c2) ∧ ist(c2, p) ⊃ ist(c1, p)
  "This gives nonmonotonic inheritance of ist in both from the subcontext to the supercontext and vice versa. More useful is the case when the sentences must change when lifted" (p.4).
- **Worked lifting (Section 4, pp.6-8).** above-theory is a context holding a static blocks-world theory, e.g. (∀xy)(on(x,y) ⊃ above(x,y)) and transitivity of above (eqs.2-4, p.6). To use it in a context c where on and above take a situation argument, c gets bridge axioms (∀xys)(on(x,y,s) ≡ ist(c1(s), on(x,y))) and the same for above (eqs.5-6, p.7), plus (∀p s)(ist(above-theory, p) ⊃ ist(c1(s), p)) (eq.8, p.7): "the facts of above-theory all hold in the contexts associated with situations". From on(A,B,S0) the derivation enters c1(S0), applies the theory, leaves, and gets above(A,B,S0) in c (eqs.9-17, pp.7-8). McCarthy adds that this "necessarily involves quantifying into an ist" (Genesereth, p.7), that "Contexts depending on parameters will surely present problems requiring more study", and that he is "more attached to the derivation than to any specific logical system" (p.8).
- **Parts of a system.** A wire's signal gets its own context c_wire117(t), lifted by ∀t(ist(c_wire117(t), signal = 0) ≡ door-open(t)): "we can introduce contexts associated with particular parts of a circuit or other system, each with its special language, and lift sentences from this context to sentences meaningful for the system as a whole" (pp.4-5).
- **Entering and leaving (Section 3, pp.5-6).** From ist(c, p) one may "enter the context c and infer the sentence p"; after inferring q inside, "one can leave c and get ist(c, q)", like discharging an assumption (p.5). Quantifier domains per context: present(c, x), or "domain(c)" (pp.5-6). McCarthy is "presently doubtful" that program reasoning "will correspond closely to using an interactive theorem prover" (p.6). Nested ist is used without reification, a problem he plans "to fix ... later" (p.6).
- **Transcendence (Section 5, pp.8-9).** Going outward from c0 to c−1. Its point is "when we want the transcending context to relax or change some assumptions of the old", e.g. an adjacency language that "may implicitly assume a gravitational field" (p.9). Within a fixed outer context the logic "could remain ordinary first order logic"; transcending the outermost "seems to require a changed logic" with reflexion principles (p.9). "The heuristics of transcendence, i.e. when a system should transcend its outer context and how, is entirely an open subject" (p.9).
- **Relative decontextualization (Section 6, pp.9-10).** In place of Quine's "eternal sentences": "when several contexts occur in a discussion, there is a common context above all of them into which all terms and predicates can be lifted. Sentences in this context are 'relatively eternal'", and further adaptation "may result in this context being transcended" (pp.9-10).
- **Mental states (Section 7, p.10).** Reasons for a belief are kept in an outer sentence, believe(p, because ...); the system reasons inside an inner context with the bare p, and a conclusion reached there "needs to acquire a suitable pedigree" on leaving (p.10).
- **Microtheories and integration (Section 8, p.11).** "Guha has put contexts into Cyc, largely in the form of microtheories. The above-theory example is a microtheory" (p.11). Air Force and General Electric engine-price databases whose prices rest on "different assumptions about spare parts and warranty conditions" get one context each, plus a joint context-GE-AF-engine-prices whose language "allows statements with auxiliary information about what is included in the price"; "Suitable lifting rules allow translating the sentences of the two other databases into this more comprehensive context" (p.11).
- **Stated limits.** New contexts come by specializing time, place, situation, subject matter, assumptions or conversation, or by transcending; "These are too many ways of getting new contexts to be treated separately" (p.11). "Useful nonmonotonic rules for lifting will surely be more complex than the examples given" (p.12). The notes are "tentative in not proposing a single language" (p.1).
- **Lineage (M93).** The notation is adopted from Guha because "he built his into Cyc" (p.2); "Guha wrote his thesis ... while this article was going through many versions ... and the mutual influences cannot be specified" (p.12). References (pp.12-13): Buvač & Mason 1993, Guha 1991, Ketonen & Weening 1984 (EKL), McCarthy & Hayes 1969, McCarthy 1979a, 1979b, 1987, 1990, Quine 1969, Shoham 1991. No Farmer, Guttman, Thayer, IMPS, Goguen, Burstall or theory-interpretation work.

### [MB98] McCarthy & Buvač, Formalizing Context (Expanded Notes)

- **Version.** Title page dated "February 28, 2012" (a re-typeset compile, p.1). "This is an expanded and revised version of [42]. An earlier version of this paper is the Stanford University Technical Note STAN-CS-TN-94-13. The current version contains new sections §7 and §13" (p.2). Footnote 1 thanks van Benthem, Costello, Fikes, Genesereth, Giunchiglia, Guha, Mason, Talcott (p.1).
- **Basic relations.** ist(c, p) and value(c, e), "designating the value of the term e in the context c"; "lifting formulas ... relate the propositions and terms in subcontexts to possibly more general propositions and terms in the outer context. Subcontexts are often specialized with regard to time, place and terminology" (p.1). Sections 1-3 repeat [M93]'s examples (pp.2-6).
- **Changed wording on specializes.** "We say specializes(c1, c2) when c2 involves no more assumptions than c1" (p.5); the translatability clause of [M93] p.4 moves to the case "when the sentences must change when lifted. Then we need to state that ... every proposition meaningful in c1 is translatable into one meaningful in c2. See §4 for an example" (p.5). The two nonmonotonic ab1/ab2 inheritance rules are as in [M93] (p.5).
- **Enter/exit as a stack.** From c0 : ist(c, p) enter c and infer c : p; from c : p exit and infer c0 : ist(c, p). "the logic needs to be such that the system always exits into the context it was in before entering. The enter and exit operations can be thought of as the push and pop operations on a stack" (p.6). Modal-logic alternatives to reifying ist are cited as [52, 9, 4, 59] (p.7).
- **Lifting axioms defined.** "Lifting axioms are axioms which relate the truth in one context to the truth in another context. Lifting is the process of inferring what is true in one context based on what is true in another context by the means of lifting axioms. We treat lifting as an informal notion in the sense that we never introduce a lifting operator" (p.7).
- **Importing a small theory into a larger context (Section 4, pp.7-9).** above-theory (axioms (1)-(3)) is used in a context blocks written in situation calculus. Bridge axioms (4)-(5): blocks : (∀xys)(on(x,y,s) ≡ ist(spec-sit(s), on(x,y))), same for above (p.8). "The importation of above-theory is expressed by the axiom" (6): c0 : (∀p) ist(above-theory, p) ⊃ ist(blocks, (∀s)(ist(spec-sit(s), p))), "asserting that the facts of above-theory all hold in the contexts associated with every situation" (p.8). Theorem (above): blocks : (∀sxy)(on(x,y,s) ⊃ above(x,y,s)) (p.8). "The example given is so small that it would be simpler to give the relations among the three-argument predicates directly, but imagine that above-theory was much larger than is given here" (p.8). Hilbert-style proof (7)-(18) by entering spec-sit(s), applying the theory, exiting, and using the ⇐ direction of (5) (p.9).
- **Logics of context.** "the drafts of this paper have motivated a number of researchers to develop logics of context, in which (some version of) the above argument would be a derivation; these include [25, 45, 9, 5]. However, at present we are more attached to the derivation than to any specific logical system" (p.10).
- **Natural deduction via context (Section 5, pp.10-12).** assuming(c, p) validates importation (c : p ⊃ q ⊢ assuming(c,p) : q) and discharge (assuming(c,p) : q ⊢ c : p ⊃ q); importation can be replaced by the lifting axiom (19) (∀cpq)(ist(c, p ⊃ q) ⊃ ist(assuming(c,p), q)); derived rule assumption ⊢ assuming(c,p) : p; universal generalization must be restricted so the variable "does not occur free in any of the assuming(c, p) terms of the current context" (p.10). The same Theorem (above) is reproved this way, (20)-(37) (pp.11-12).
- **Postponing preconditions (5.1, p.13).** From c : have-ticket(x) ∧ clothed(x) ⊃ can-fly(x), importation gives assuming(c, clothed(x)) : have-ticket(x) ⊃ can-fly(x), so the precondition is set aside. "we are only developing an ontology for representing this phenomena, and are not dealing with pragmatic issues like which context a reasoning system will start in, and how the system will decide to consider a context making an additional assumption"; pragmatically the process may run in reverse: the system "may realize that its current problem solving context c is making a particular assumption p that needs to be discharged" and look for c′ with c = assuming(c′, p) (p.13).
- **Integrating databases (Section 6, pp.13-19).** Two databases "not originally intended to be used together, can be integrated by lifting their contents into a wider context" (p.13). GE, Navy and Air Force prices differ in hidden assumptions: GE retail without spares; Navy government price including spares; Air Force including inventory costs and a different spares assortment (p.14). A problem-solving context c_ps holds objects c_GE, c_N, c_AF; a GE fact becomes ist(c_GE, price(FX-22-engine) = $3600K), true in c_ps (p.14). "Different data bases might make different assumptions"; functions spares(c, x) and warranty(c, x) name what each context "assumes necessary and thus includes in the price" (pp.14-15). These assumptions "will probably not be contained in the Navy data base ... Rather, this information is kept in some manual. But for these data bases to be used jointly, the spares information needs to be included; we assume that it is described declaratively in c_ps" (p.15). Lifting axioms (40)-(42) "define how the notion of price in different databases translates into the problem solving context", e.g. (41) value(c_NAVY, price(x)) = GE-price(x) + GE-price(spares(c_NAVY,x)) + GE-price(warranty(c_NAVY,x)) (p.15); the Air Force's f "might not be precisely known, in which case we might decide to only give some approximate bounds on f" (p.15). Theorem (engine price): c_NAVY : price(FX-22-engine) = $3611K, proved by exiting c_GE, computing in c_ps, entering c_NAVY (pp.16-17). Stated assumption: "all constants denote the same object in all contexts, i.e. that all constants are rigid designators" (p.17).
- **Lifting that constrains without computing (6.3, pp.17-18).** In the bargaining version, lifting axioms (58)-(59) map list price and budget price to engine-only prices; "given the list price the lifting axioms can not be used to work out the budget price. The lifting axioms simply ensure that both the list price and the budget price talk only about the engine, and do not implicitly assume the inclusion of spare parts" (p.18).
- **value as abbreviation and domains (6.4-6.5, pp.18-19).** (60) value(c,x) = y ≡ (∀z)(y = z ≡ ist(c, x = z)) when all contexts share domains (p.18). "Not all objects will typically exist in all contexts": E(c, x); with differing domains a precondition (E(c,x) ∧ E(c,y)) ⊃ ... is added (64), "The main implication connective in this formula might not be classical" (p.19); (66) c0 : (∀c) involved-in-ps(c) ⊃ (∀x)(E(c,x) ⊃ E(c_ps, x)) (p.19). "the classical rule of universal instantiation is not valid for non-rigid terms. Logics of context which allow functions, will thus need to have a restricted form of universal instantiation" (p.19).
- **Combining planning contexts (Section 7, from p.20).** "the techniques we have used for combining data bases or knowledge bases (§4-§6) can be directly applied in combining of plans produced by different planners using different languages" (p.20). "represent each subplan as though it was developed in a context ... The terminology within each subcontext is likely to be specialized, and making the plans work together requires some generalization" via lifting formulas (p.20). Example: a route planner with no notion of time or supplies and a supply planner with no notion of routes (p.20). Lifting axiom (70) ps : ist(supply_planner, transport(x,l1,d1,l2,d2)) ⊃ transport(x, value(route_planner, route(l1,l2)), d1, d2); "the same predicate symbol, transport, is used in different ways in two different contexts: its arity and its arguments are different" (p.21). "A term with a definite meaning in one context often needs translation when used in another context. Thus Rome may mean Rome NY in a data base of US Air Force bases but needs translation when a formula is lifted to a context of worldwide geography" (p.21).
- **Kindness assumptions (7.2, pp.22-23).** "Any plan produced from the lifting axiom 70 makes numerous assumptions", e.g. that the shortest path arrives on time. Such "kindness assumptions" "amount to assuming that the world is kind ... They allow us to focus on the aspects of a plan that seem to be relevant to the problem at hand and to disregard details which we assume will hold for that particular problem class. However, whenever kindness assumptions are made it is important to have a mechanism which enables us to discharge such assumptions and reason about their validity in cases when it is unclear whether they hold" (p.22). In ps_urgent the assumption becomes an explicit antecedent: lifting axiom (72) ist(ps, transport(x,r,d1,d2)) ⊃ timely_route(d1,d2,r) ⊃ transport(x,r,d1,d2); (73) combines (70) and (72) (pp.22-23).
- **Heterogeneous objects (7.3, p.23).** "objects that were not designed to work together ... may be databases of facts, programs, hardware or plans. Even if the objects were intended to be used together, maintenance of adherence to their specifications and ensuring the consistency of terminology through time is often difficult. Each object was developed in a context, and these contexts differ, either slightly or greatly" (p.23).
- **Discourse (Section 8, pp.23-29).** Discourse contexts are "characterized by the sentences which are true in them but also by the intended meaning of their predicates, which might vary from one discourse context to the next" (p.23). query(c, φ) and reply(c, φ) give successive contexts; axioms: ist(query(c,φ), φ ≡ yes); frame axiom ist(c,ψ) ⊃ ist(query(c,φ),ψ) when yes is not in ψ; qualitative versions with answer(x); reply axiom ist(reply(c,φ),ψ) ≡ ist(c, φ ⊃ ψ) (p.25). The system is assumed "given the discourse utterances in the form of logical formulas ... the process of producing this theory is not precisely defined, and it is not completely clear how much common sense information is needed to generate it. It might turn out that producing such a theory requires the solution of the problem we had set out to solve" (p.24). Air Force-GE dialogue (bid, $4M, "Does that include spares?" ### [G91] Guha, Contexts: A Formalization and Some Applications

- **The problem it starts from.** The standard KB model treats sentences as Quine's "eternal or universal sentences, i.e., all foreseeable dependencies (of the sentence) have been made explicit in the sentence" (pp.3-4). KBs are homogeneous: "The same vocabulary is used uniformly throughout the KB"; "Typically, the KB contains a single theory of the world. To allow meaningful inference, this theory must be kept consistent"; and that single theory "should be kept independent of particular problems" (pp.4-5). "Most of these problems mentioned here are from experiences gathered in trying to build the Cyc knowledge base" (p.5).
- **Vocabulary choice is an assumption.** "The choice of the vocabulary can therefore exclude certain phenomena from consideration by the KB. If certain phenomena are excluded, this constitutes an assumption that these phenomena are not important/do not exist ... It is almost inevitable that certain parts of the domain will be overlooked in the representation process" (p.5). Worked example: (Buy X Y Z) cannot tell repeated sales apart; adding time cannot tell simultaneous agent deals apart; reifying events is "more expressive" but "significantly more cumbersome"; a single price still excludes non-monetary obligations (pp.5-6). "with a little searching, we can always uncover inexpressible phenomenon. At some point, we have to finalize the vocabulary"; "There is a certain 'upward compatibility' of our vocabularies which our programs should have. The first step towards designing these upwardly compatible vocabularies is to capture the fact that the choice of the vocabulary incorporates certain assumptions" (p.6).
- **Theories make assumptions too, and some objects are out of scope rather than exceptions.** For a buyer acting for no reason, "almost none of the axioms in the theory would be applicable to him. It is not as though the individual rules in the theory are defaults and this particular buyer is an exception : He is simply beyond the scope of the theory" (p.7). The standard fix (a very rich vocabulary, make every assumption explicit) is "not adequate as the only solution": limitations will be found later and may require "a reworking of relevant parts of KB", and a broad vocabulary is "cumbersome" for both writing and inference; yet "a naive theory of buying and selling ... is certainly very useful. In fact, this theory is all we need for most purposes" (p.7).
- **What a context (microtheory) is.** "The information associated with a naive theory of commercial transactions includes not just the axioms ... but also information about the assumptions made by the theory, when these assumptions are reasonable, when this theory is applicable, etc. These are 'external' to the axioms constituting the theory itself" (p.7). Axiom A1 of NaiveMoneyMt is written ist(NaiveMoneyMt A1) (p.7). Contexts "are objects in the domain ... They are rich objects ... a context cannot be completely described" (p.8). The context "is supposed to capture everything that is not in A1 that is required to make A1 a meaningful statement representing what it is intended to state. This includes assumptions made by A1 about the domain, assumptions about the applicability of A1, how A1 might relate to statements in other contexts, etc."; "the context object can be thought of as the reification of context dependencies of the sentences associated with the context" (p.8). The robot picture: writing a representation is a function of the domain and also of "resource constraints on the robot, the parts of teh domain that escaped the attention of the robot, the duty the robot was performing"; "the context argument accounts for that fact that there were these other factors influencing the representation. In a sense, we are in a position similar to that of the robot" (p.8). Footnote: "This concept of contexts was first introduced by McCarthy in [Advice Taker] [Generality In AI]" (p.8).
- **What contexts must support (pp.8-10).** "if all we could do with this new syntax was to partition the KB into separate theories, that would not be very useful" (p.8). Wanted:
  - (a) scope: ¬presentIn(NaiveMoneyMt Fred) for an object outside the theory; by default "maximize its scope", by maximizing presentIn (p.9).
  - (b) statements about classes of theories: (forall c NaiveTheory(c) ⇒ ist(c (forall x Human(x) ⇒ Rational(x)))) (p.9).
  - (c) relating theories with different vocabularies: (∀x ist(NaiveMoneyMt cost(x) = A) ⇔ ist(GTT cost(x List) = A)), where cost is unary in one and binary in the other; "The two contexts use different vocabularies and make different attributions of an object, but these attributions are about the same object"; "This process is also called Lifting"; "This process of relative decontextualization is a substitute for complete decontextualization that is assumed by the traditional model" (pp.9-10).
  - (d) mutually inconsistent contexts: ist(C1 cost(g) = A) and ist(C2 cost(g) = B), one with sales tax; "the KB as a whole should not become inconsistent. We should only be required to maintain local consistency within each contexts and not necessarily across contexts" (p.10).
  - (e) focus: enter C1 so "the only axioms used by the system are P1, P2, ..."; exit to get ist(C1 q) (p.10).
  - (f) "Given a problem, we might not have a context with just the right theory ... We should be able to create a new context, lift the relevant axioms from these two theories into this context, enter this context and solve the problem ... The lifting process needs to perform the requisite relative decontextualization" (p.10). "Traditionally, research on problem solving has assumed that the representation (theory) is a given and has been set up before the problem solving starts. The goal of the create, lift, enter, problem solve, exit sequence is to bring the choice of the theory used to solve a problem within the scope of the problem solving" (p.10).

- **Translations that keep their context (1.3.5, pp.11-14).** The traditional NL front end must produce "the completely decontextualized translation"; this assumes "the context dependency of natural language utterances is completely accountable by linguistic knowledge", but "NL utterances often have many non-linguistic assumptions and it is difficult for the front end to uncover these" (p.11). Instead, the "literal translation" F is added as ist(C1 F), where C1 is the context of that text, "supposed to account for the context dependencies remaining in the translation" (pp.11-12). Model-kit example: assembly instructions and emergency instructions (child swallowing adhesive) make "radically different" assumptions, and adding both literal translations without contexts lets the system "arrive at unintended conclusions" (p.12). "the system should also be given information about C1 ... Based on this information about C1, the context dependencies of F1 can be made explicit asynchronously, later on demand ... By bringing the context dependency within the realm of the KB, all the knowledge and tools available in the KB can be used to reveal and exploit these dependencies" (p.12). Travel advisory: a preamble "during winter" qualifies every statement in C2; other qualifications are unstated (p.12). "As the scope of context is made finer, the class of contextual phenomena which can be retained in the translation increases" (pp.12-13). Many inferences need no full decontextualization ("He is an engineer" answers "has he gone to school?" without resolving "He"), and "inference using the contextual translation is usually much simpler" (p.13).
- **One machinery, many axiom sets (1.4, p.14).** The problems "share a common origin, i.e., the intended universal and decontextualized nature of expressions used in representation", so one logical machinery may serve; "The other part of the solution is in the form of axioms describing and relating contexts. The kind of context related axioms required could vary significantly from one problem to another" (p.14). Chapter 3 holds "examples from the use of contexts in the Cyc system" and "much of the information in this thesis which a person interested in using contexts would find useful"; Appendix A is a script of a Cyc application using contexts (p.15).
- **Desired logical properties (2.1.1, pp.17-20).** (1) contexts are first-class objects and ist formulas first-class formulas, including quantification over contexts and nesting (p.17). (2) "Different contexts could use different languages ... F might be meaningful in a context C1 but not in a context C2", e.g. "vis viva" meaningful in sixteenth-century mechanics, not in modern (pp.17-18); so "we need to distinguish between meaningfulness and grammaticality ... Each context defines its own language (in the sense of first order logic)" (p.18). (3) "We should be able to 'embed' first order theories in framework of contexts ... if γ ⊨_FOL α, then γ ⊨_LOC α should be true irrespective of which context γ is in ... deriving α from γ (using FOL proof theory) in a context C should be equivalent to deriving ist(C α) from ist(C γ). The reason for imposing this requirement is to allow us to use existing FOL based representations and problem solvers with contexts"; this gives "the theory associated with a context, i.e., the deductive closure (under the rules of FOL) of the set of formulas α such that ist(c α)" (p.18). (4) ist is referentially opaque: "A symbol might denote different things in different contexts"; "A symbol might be used as a predicate in one context, individual constant in another, and function in yet another" (pp.18-19). (5) nesting: the vis viva axiom ist(C1 (mass(m1) = mass(m2) ∧ vv(x) > vv(y))) ⇒ ist(C2 ((mom(x) > mom(y)) ∧ (en(x) > en(y)))) "is meta to both these theories, i.e., is not in either theory, but is a statement relating the two theories" (p.19). (6) being in a context: the outermost context stays implicit; entering C1 means "acting as if the only axioms in the system are α, where ist(C1 α) is true is in the outer context" (p.20).
- **Model theory (2.1.2, pp.20-23).** A "language is a subset of the total vocabulary" with each symbol designated predicate, function or individual constant and given a fixed arity; a model of a language is a FOL model ("This definition of a model is the same as that of FOL") (p.21). "A context structure CS is a function that assigns to each context C - A Language L(C) - A set of structures CM(C). Intuitively, a context structure defines a context's way of describing the world. L defines the language/ vocabulary, and CM defines the theory of the world according to the context" (p.21). F is meaningful in C under CS iff every symbol outside an ist is in L(C) and used with the right kind and arity (p.21). ⟨U, CS, S⟩ satisfies F in C iff U ∈ CM(C), F is meaningful in C, and the FOL truth rules hold; ist(ck P) is satisfied iff Ck is a context and for every U_j in CM(Ck), P is satisfied in Ck by ⟨U_j, CS, S⟩ (p.22). Entailment in C: every satisfying tuple of F in C satisfies G in C (p.23). Defined predicates: presentIn(c A) ⇔ (∀z (= A z) ⇒ ist(c (∃y z = y))), which cannot be simplified to ist(c (∃y A = y)) unless "all contexts have the same domain ... and all ground terms denote the same objects in all contexts"; corefer(C1 A C2 B) ⇔ (∀z ist(C1 (= A z)) ⇔ ist(C2 (= B z))), e.g. corefer(C1 Now C2 TheYear1865) for Lincoln's speech; value(c A) (p.23). Contexts may be restricted to a DAG by (∀ci ¬presentIn(ci ci)) (p.23). "The above logic is monotonic and we will need nonmonotonicity ... We will use circumscription (pointwise, prioritized). All predicates starting with 'ab' will be assumed to be minimized ... (Note : The implementation in Cyc uses Argumentation Based Default Reasoning.)" (p.23).
- **Discussion of the semantics (pp.24-25).** Meaningless statements are neither true nor false: "If F is meaningless in all consistent interpretations of c, we have ¬ist(c F) ∧ ¬ist(c ¬F)"; ist(c F) itself stays meaningful ("just false") (p.24). "The treatment of meaninglessness is very limited": bald(King-of(France)) "could be meaningful just because King-of, bald and France, are all part of the vocabulary of the context" (p.24). Domains may differ, so the Barcan formula fails; "For the rest of this thesis, I will assume that the domain of of the outer context is larger than the domain of the inner contexts" (p.24). Non-axioms: ist(c p ∨ q) ⇒ ist(c p) ∨ ist(c q) and ¬ist(c p) ⇒ ist(c ¬p) hold only if the context's theory "was known to be complete" (p.24). "The implementation has only one level of nesting" (p.24). Outer/meta versus general: in the vis viva axiom, "c0 is 'outer' or 'meta to' both C1 and C2, but C2 is more 'general' than C1. The generality relation between contexts is not defined by the logic, but the meta relation is" (p.25).
- **Proof theory (2.1.3, pp.25-27).** Each proof line carries a context sequence; lines are formulas, Enter ci, or Exit (pp.25-26). "If we constrain the proof to not contain Enter or Exit, the above definition reduces to the definition of a proof in first order logic"; "Though all these rules are sound I don't know whether the logic is complete" (p.26). Rules: full FOL inside formulas without ist; ist(Ci α) LC ist(Ci γ) / ist(Ci α LC γ); "Modus Ponens from outside" (enter, apply, exit); ist(ci α) / ¬ist(ci ¬α); Skolemization (p.26). "Since ist is referentially opaque, the first order version of universal instantiation does not hold"; the modified rule needs A = value(C1 A1) = value(C2 A2) ... (p.27). ist(c ∀x γ(x)) / (∀x presentIn(c x) ⇒ ist(c γ(x))); the converse and a further rule hold only under the larger-outer-domain assumption (p.27).
- **Example lifting axioms (2.2, pp.28-29)**, described as dealing with "some of the standard syntactically characterizable differences between contexts" (p.28):
  1. dropping an argument to a predicate: (∀x ist(C1 Tall(x)) ⇔ ist(C2 Tall(x Person))) (p.28);
  2. dropping an argument to a function, guarded: (∀x,y ¬(ab-cost C1 C2 x) ⇒ ist(C1 cost(x)=y) ⇔ ist(C2 (∀z cost(x z)=y))); "The context C1 may be used only if everyone associates the same value for that object" (p.28);
  3. an assumption about the domain: C1 (house fixtures) "Assumes that all fixtures all installed in houses", C2 does not; (∀x ist(C2 Fixture(x) ∧ ¬installed(x)) ⇔ ¬presentIn(x C1)); from ist(C1 (∀x LightSocket(x) ⇒ ∃y connectedTo(x y) ∧ Wire(y))) one derives ist(C2 (∀x (Fixture(x) ⇒ installed(x)) ⇒ (LightSocket(x) ⇒ ∃y connectedTo(x y) ∧ Wire(y)))) (p.28): in the more general context the assumption becomes an explicit antecedent;
  4. fixing time: ¬(ab-time ci ti) ⇒ ist(fix-time(ci ti) p) ⇔ ist(ci holds(p ti)), "[not an axiom but a schema]" (p.29);
  5. indexicals: corefer(C1 Now C2 T1) with (∀x ist(C1 holds(p x)) ⇒ ist(C2 holds(p x))) gives ist(C2 holds(p T1)) (p.29).
- **Sample proof (2.3, pp.29-30).** With (A1) ist(C1 Tall(x)) ⇒ ist(C2 Tall(x Person)) and facts in each context, the query ist(C2 height(Fred) > Feet 5) does not follow at first: "The information given is actually inadequate ... This is because the symbol 'Fred' could potentially refer to different people in C1 and C2. So we need the additional axiom, (A5) value(C1 Fred) = value(C2 Fred)" (pp.29-30). Then the proof enters C2, reasons, exits (p.30). The fixtures proof uses a further supplied axiom (A3) ist(C1 P(x)) ∧ presentIn(C2 x) ⇒ ist(C2 P(x)) and ends with (D5) ist(C2 (∀x q(x) ⇒ P(x))) (p.31).
- **What lifting is (2.4, pp.31-32).** Using formulas in one context to conclude formulas in another "involved performing a relative decontextualization of the formula, i.e. the differences between the origin and target context had to be taken into account to obtain a formula with the same truth conditions in both the contexts. This procedure is called lifting and the axioms which enable us to do this are called lifting rules" (pp.31-32). Both proofs needed a supplied bridge axiom ((A5) same denotation of Fred; (A3) same truth value of p(x)); "It seems that the number of axioms required of this sort is going to be very large. We need some general lifting axioms" (p.32). "Given a pair of contexts (C1 and C2) and a formula F1 in C1, we would like to determine what an equivalent formula in C2 would be ... 'state the same thing' ... For the present, this concept of 'states the same thing' will be simplified to a biconditional" (p.32); flower-pot example ("to your left" becomes "to your right" after Chris moves) (p.32).
- **General lifting framework (2.4.2, pp.32-35).** "in general we expect that there will be a significant similarity and overlap between contexts ... if a lifting is not going to involve any change to the formula under consideration, we should not have to write an explicit lifting rule" (pp.32-33). Compositionality: "Rather than specifying lifting rules for each formula separately, we should be able to specify lifting rules for individual predicates (and functions) and formulas involving these predicates (and functions) should get lifted automatically based on this specification ... The lifting rules will specify how these vocabularies can be mapped between each other" (p.33). Two components:
  - (a) the Default Coreference Rule: "as a default, the meaning of a symbol does not change from one context to another". DCR-T: (∀ci cj ¬ab-dtc(S ci cj)) ⇒ (value(ci S) = value(cj S)), "a dual of the unique names assumption"; one such axiom per symbol, since S cannot be quantified, and "In practice, as with the UNA, the problem solver is modified to deal with this" (p.33). For predicates, equality of extensions as a default would collapse at the first exception, so DCR-P states "maximal intersection": (∀ci cj P X ¬ab-dcp(P ci cj X) ⇒ (ist(ci P(X)) ⇔ ist(cj P(X)))), likewise DCR-F (p.34). These lift atomic formulas, conjunctions and negations; ∀x P(x) lifts unchanged only if the two domains are the same (p.34).
  - (b) the composition rule CR: if costate(ci α1 cj α2) and costate(ci γ1 cj γ2) then costate(ci (α1 LC γ1) cj (α2 LC γ2)), with costate(ci α cj γ) ≡ ist(ci α) ⇔ ist(cj γ) (p.34). "the above axioms provide only a framework and in some sense, allow for very 'promiscuous' lifting across contexts. In the next chapter we will cover several restrictions where these lifting axioms are blocked" (p.35).
- **Lifting example (2.5, pp.35-36).** From ist(C1 (∀x Plays(x BasketBall) ⇒ Tall(x))) and DCR with no exceptions, ist(C2 (∀x Plays(x BasketBall) ⇒ Tall(x))) (p.35). When C2 gains a finer vocabulary (professional versus backyard basketball) and "C1 implicitly assumed that we were talking about Professional participation", the single axiom (A2) ist(C1 Plays(x BasketBall)) ⇔ ist(C2 Plays(x BasketBall Professional)) yields (D6) ist(C2 (∀x Plays(x BasketBall Professional) ⇒ Tall(x))): "based on (A2) which made no mention of Tall or (A1), the appropriate modification to (A1) can be derived" (p.36). ab-dcp holds when an argument is not presentIn C2, "p is not in the vocabulary of C2", or "The arity of p, or the argument types of p are different in C2" (p.36).
- **Model-theoretic account (2.6, from p.36).** A background theory B is associated with each utterance U; a theory "specifies a set of possibilities ... the set of models that satisfy it"; "The information conveyed by the new axiom/ formula U with respect to the background theory B, is the set of models eliminated from B by the addition of U to B" (pp.36-37). "Everyone is wearing a tie" under a background theory restricting humans to those in the room conveys only that those in the room wear ties; "Given the background theory, the two statements are the same. Without the theory, they are very different" (p.37). Relative decontextualization: a person called outside must report "everyone in the room was wearing a tie", because the new background theory includes a secretary; "the only possibilities that the questioners theory has which are eliminated by the speakers utterance are the ones which claim that the speaker and P1 are not wearing ties" (p.37).
- **Semantic definition of relative decontextualization (pp.38-39).** F1 uttered against theory T1 eliminates T11 ⊆ T1. To say what F2 must eliminate from T2, "we have to assume that the relation and function tuples of the models in T11 can be mapped onto the relation and function tuples of the models in T2. This mapping is called the correspondence function (CF)"; CF may be partial (p.38). The restriction UD of a model U by a domain D keeps only tuples inside D (p.38). "A model UT2 is eliminated from T2 iff there is a model UT11 in T11 such that" the domains overlap non-trivially and, after restricting both to the overlap D, CF maps each relation of UTD11 to the same relation in UTD2 (p.38). F2 is the formula removing exactly those models (p.38). If F1 adds nothing, no UT2 is eliminated; if F1 contradicts T2's background, every model of T2 is eliminated; "it is possible that not all the information conveyed by F1 in T1 is captured by F2", because of the narrow scope of T2 (p.38); and there may be no decontextualization at all ("Someone is wearing a tie") (p.38). For a KB, "the assumptions made by a context define a theory which serves as the background theory for the axioms in that context" (p.39).
- **Relation of semantics to lifting rules (2.6.4, p.39).** "The above analysis has been completely at the model theory level. It gives us no clues as to how one might go about generating the formula F2 ... We assumed that we are given this mapping function F. There is the obvious problem that we simply don't have any such function available." A relation may correspond to "a relation with a different arity or some complex constraint between relations", so models must first be "normalized" (p.39). The DCR and other lifting rules "specify the F function" and handle ground atomic formulas; CR handles non-atomic ground formulas; rules (i)-(l) "take care of the restriction of models (based on differences in the domain) and take care of the lifting of the quantifiers" (p.39). "Informally, the lifting framework defines the proof theory for the semantic definition of relative decontextualization" (p.39). No soundness or completeness theorem linking the two is stated in the pages read.
- **Costatement is not a biconditional (2.7, p.40).** Writing ist(C1 A) ⇔ ist(C2 B) "is inadequate for two reasons": it is weaker than costatement (if C2 knows the planet count is 9, ist(C1 (> 10 no-of-planets)) ⇔ ist(C2 (> 10 9)) holds but "(> 10 9) certainly does not state the same thing"), and it can say more than wanted (a C1 statement whose content is expressible in C2 but false there) (p.40). "Pragmatically, the problem is that we might not know much about the theory associated with C2 when writing the lifting rules ... by equating costatement with biconditionals, the distinction between costatement and truth conditions is lost. We cheat and use defaults as a way of getting out of this problem ... What to lift (assuming the lifting can be done) is stated using default biconditionals. When the lifting may or may not be done is stated by specifying when the default does not hold" (p.40).
- **Uses of contexts (3.1, from p.41).** "Implementing the logical machinery is only a small fraction of the effort involved in building a context based system. The bulk of the effort lies in writing the rules for describing and interelating contexts ... the structure and content of these rules is heavily dependent on the kind of use" (p.41). Use (1a): "a theory of some topic, e.g., a theory of mechanics, a theory of the weather in winter, a theory of what to look for when buying cars, etc. Contexts used in this sense are called 'Microtheories' (Mts) and usually have the suffix Mt. Different microtheories might make different assumptions and simplifications about the world with contexts providing a mechanism for recording and reasoning using these assumptions" (p.41). Use (1b): "a representation (of some situation) that is tailored for the problem it was set up to solve, e.g., a model of a christmas tree as a perfect cone, used for determining whether it will fit in a given box; a model of an object as a point mass for determining its trajectory" (p.41). Such contexts "are called Problem Solving Contexts (PSC)"; (1c) discourse contexts keep "very slightly decontextualized" utterances, e.g. "(The Person)" (p.42).
- **Why bundle assertions into named contexts (p.42).** "Assumptions that are reasonable only for limited domains may be made. These in turn allow simplification of the statement of the theory"; a small relevant subset gives the problem solver "focus"; "By naming theories, they allow for statements about a theory (regarding its utility, reliability, precision, etc.) to be made"; "By keeping different theories distinct, the problem of maintaining consistency is transformed from that of maintaining global consistency to maintaining local consistency, which is arguably a simpler problem" (p.42).
- **Use (2): combining theories (pp.42-43).** "If the assertions in one context were not automatically available (as appropriate) in other contexts, the system might as well be a set of disconnected knowledge bases" (p.42). "Queries posed to the KB are also likely to make their own set of assumptions. When querying the KB, the question should be posed in a Problem Solving Context that captures the assumptions made by the question" (p.42). "The assumptions made by the Problem Solving Context could be different from the assumptions made by the contexts from which the information is lifted. These differences need to be 'factored in' ... The determination of which contexts from which to lift and the 'factoring in' of these differences in assumptions is taken care of by the lifting rules" (p.43). Cyc example: a workplace-behaviour microtheory assumes people follow the workplace code of conduct; with a small child brought into the office, the rule should apply to the parent but not the child; "The lifting rules should appropriately modify assertions so they apply only to parts of a situation that satisfy the assumptions" (p.43). When more assumptions of that microtheory are discovered later (rationality, a reason for being in the office), the system "should be able to infer that the parent is rational, has a reason for being in the office" (p.43).
- **Use (3): limiting the scope of a theory (pp.43-44).** "Common sense theories are almost never universally applicable. The objects and situations within the purview of a theory (i.e., the scope of the universal quantifier), are determined by the assumptions made by the theory" (p.43); the code-of-conduct assumption "restricts the scope of the theory to exclude children ... This assumption simplifies the theory and could either have been made by design or could have been made unintentionally but then later noticed and made explicit" (p.44). "at any stage of building and using a knowledge base, a significant number of assumptions made by the microtheories in that knowledge base will not have been made explicit. But if these assumptions have been made by almost all the contexts in the system, it might not be very important to make them explicit"; e.g. that making loud noises is under conscious control, "satisfied in almost every situation the system might encounter ... What matters is that when the scope of Cyc is increased to a point where it knows about phenomenon such as individuals spontaneously making loud noises, it can be told that this assumption has been made all along" (p.44).
- **Use (4): multiple models of one situation (pp.44-45).** "Many situations are able to be modeled using different representations with each making a different tradeoff between efficiency, simplicity and accuracy" (Navier-Stokes versus simpler flow equations; paths out of a room from cuboid obstacles up to wind resistance) (p.44). "none of these theories is a priori true or false. We need to replace the concept of a theory being true with the concept of a theory being adequate for the problem we are trying to solve" (p.44). "these theories are often mutually contradictory ... Sometimes, both theories are equally 'correct' and a choice must be made as to which theory to use in which instance ... it is not reasonable to require consistency across theories" (p.45). "Contexts keep these theories apart. They also enable us to represent the concept of a theory being suitable for some task but not necessarily all tasks. Given a knowledge base with many of these theories, the system must determine which single theory or which combination of theories to use to solve a given problem. The context mechanism is used to keep the different theories separate and the selection of the appropriate theory is specified using lifting rules. The problem of putting together a theory that is adequate for solving a given problem is often called the 'model formation' problem" (p.45).
- **Use (5): natural language and representation (pp.45-47).** Context parameters range from "very coarse grained factors such as the cultural settings" to "the immediately preceding utterance" (p.45). A "chicken and egg situation": the system needs the KB to find the context dependence of a new assertion, but needs to factor it out before adding it; "The only way out of this cycle is to add the mechanism required to allow us to add the assertion to the knowledge base without factoring out all the contextual effects" (p.46). "writing a theory (of that topic) in terms of formulas is usually preceded by writing the theory in english. This often manifests itself in some of the aspects of natural language remaining in the resulting representation"; "seller" and "USA" are polysemous, and "The mechanism of contexts is used for insulating these different meanings from each other and for translating from one to the other" (pp.46-47).
- **Lifting rules in practice (3.2, from p.47).** "the price to be paid for the use of contexts is the need to write lifting rules ... if the system required a separate lifting rule for each axiom, origin context, and target context tuple, much of the system would just consist of lifting axioms. This is clearly unacceptable" (p.47). Physicist and geologist mean different things by "fast" but "there is more in common between the two languages than is different between them ... This is what makes it feasible to write the translation/lifting rules" (p.47). DCR as implemented: "Though special classes of terms such as indexicals, definite pronouns, etc. violate this assumption, most terms do follow this default" (p.48). "However, we do not want to liberally lift assertions from one microtheory to another. Therefore, we restrict this lifting policy to lift assertions from microtheories (such as the AutoMt and HumanKinshipMt) to Problem Solving Contexts (such as CSPSC)" (p.48); default lifting is blocked when the representation of p differs in the target ("more arguments or the sort constraints") or the denoted object "is not in the domain of c2" (p.48). Compositional lifting (CR) extends the default to arbitrary formulas; "Other lifting rules which modify assertions as they are lifted will override these two defaults" (p.49).
- **Two ways to answer a query (3.3, p.49).** "Lift and Solve: The system lifts assertions from other contexts into the Problem Solving Context and uses a conventional problem solver once inside"; "Shift and Solve: The system switches into an already existing context, solves the problem in that context (using a conventional problem solver) and lifts the answer back into the context in which the query was posed" (p.49). A conventional problem solver is one for a first-order language without context mechanisms; "Once inside a context, contexts do not affect problem solving and so one of the goals is to be able to use existing problem solvers in conjunction with contexts" (p.49). The current implementation mostly uses lift-and-solve (p.49).
- **Lift and solve (3.3.1, p.50).** Running lifting rules to completion to "build" a theory in the PSC and then calling a traditional solver "has the advantage modularizing the context related problem solving steps (lifting) from the other problem solving steps", "However, this approach is infeasible in that it might take much too long for the lifting rules to run to completion. In the ideal case, the system should lift only those assertions and exactly those assertions which will be required to answer the query. Unfortunately, we have no (efficient and guaranteed) means of determining this set of axioms" (p.50). The approach keeps the solver's inferencing module and replaces its access module "with the mechanism for doing the lifting" (p.50).

### [GG01] Ghidini & Giunchiglia, Local Models Semantics (propstore copy)

- **Two principles (p.222).** "Principle 1 (of Locality). Reasoning uses only part of what is potentially available (e.g., what is known, the available inference procedures). The part being used while reasoning is what we call context (of reasoning)"; "Principle 2 (of Compatibility). There is compatibility among the kinds of reasoning performed in different contexts" (p.222). Contexts in AI "were first introduced in Weyhrauch's work on mechanizing logical theories in the FOL system [43]" and became widely discussed in the late 1980s "when they were independently proposed by Fausto Giunchiglia [25] and John McCarthy [35]"; for [25] contexts formalize "the locality of reasoning", for [35] they solve "the problem of generality" (pp.221-222). Applications cited include integration of heterogeneous knowledge and data bases (Farquhar et al., Ghidini & Serafini, Mylopoulos & Motschnig-Pitrik, Theodorakis et al.) and CYC (p.222).
- **Magic box (2.1, pp.223-224).** Two observers each see a partial view of a six-sector box. Locality: both have "right" and "left" but these differ, and Mr.2's "center" "is meaningless for Mr.1" (p.223). Compatibility: the views are related "as a consequence of the fact that Mr.1 and Mr.2 see the same box"; it can be given by listing all compatible pairs of local views (Fig. 3) or by constraints like "if Mr.1 sees at least one ball then Mr.2 sees at least one ball" (pp.223-224).
- **Why not axiomatize the world and project views (p.224).** "the most straightforward formalization of this example would be a direct axiomatization of the box as a two-dimensional grid ... Locality and compatibility would be guaranteed by construction. However this approach is based on the hypothesis that we have a complete description of the world ... and that we can use it to build views of the world itself. This is not always the case. Quite often there are only partial views and only a partial or approximate view of the world can be reconstructed"; two different box contents (Fig. 4) are indistinguishable to both observers and map to the same compatible pair (p.224). Databases are named as a domain "where we may or may not have a complete description of the world" (p.224).
- **Local models and models (3.1, pp.226-228).** {L_i} is a family of (first-order) languages, "each L_i is the (formal) language used to describe what is true in a context"; M̄_i is the class of all models of L_i, each a "local model" (p.226). "A compatibility sequence c ... is a sequence c = ⟨c_0, c_1, ..., c_i, ...⟩, where, for each i ∈ I, c_i is a subset of M̄_i"; a "compatibility relation C ... is a set C = {c} of compatibility sequences", C ⊆ ∏ 2^{M̄_i} (p.227). Definition 3.1: a model is a compatibility relation with C ≠ ∅ and ⟨∅, ∅, ...⟩ ∉ C, which "eliminate[s] ... totally inconsistent context structures" (p.227). "Local models describe what is locally true. Compatibility sequences put together local models which are 'mutually compatible', consistently with the situation we are describing" (p.227). In the magic box, restricting Mr.1 to exactly one ball "forces us to throw away all the pairs, and corresponding local models for Mr.2, which allow for zero balls" (pp.227-228).
- **Federations of databases (p.225).** "a federation of heterogeneous data or knowledge bases, possibly developed independently, can be seen as a set of views of an ideal data base which is often impossible or very complex to reconstruct completely" (p.225).
- **Chains and contexts as partial objects (3.1-3.2, pp.229-230).** Def 3.2: c is a chain if |c_i| = 1 for every i; Def 3.3: a weak chain if |c_i| ≤ 1 (p.229). "a context [is] any c_i, namely the set of local models m ∈ M̄_i allowed by C within any particular compatibility sequence ... defining a context as a set of models (instead of a single model) enables us to formalize it as a partial object ... This is a key difference with possible worlds [32], which are complete objects" (p.229). In Example 3.2 Mr.2 sees one sector and "there is a ball on the right" is "neither true nor false" in c_2 (p.229). "A context is a partial description of the world. A compatibility sequence contains as many contexts as needed, one for each partial description of the world" (p.230). A chain context "assigns a truth value to all the propositions it is able to express in its local (and limited) language"; an empty context "assigns the truth value 'true' to all sentences in its language, therefore describing an inconsistent situation" (p.230).
- **Satisfaction and consequence across languages (3.3, p.231).** Def 3.4: C ⊨ i:φ iff for all c ∈ C, every m ∈ c_i classically satisfies φ (p.231). Def 3.6: Γ ⊨_C i:φ iff every c ∈ C satisfies "∀j ∈ I, j ≠ i, c_j ⊨ Γ_j ⇒ (∀m ∈ c_i, m ⊨_cl Γ_i ⇒ m ⊨_cl φ)"; "the formulae in Γ_j prune away compatibility sequences, while the formulae in Γ_i prune away local models in c_i. This is due to the fact that the assumptions Γ_j (j ≠ i) made in the context c_j induce 'compatible' assumptions in other contexts" (p.231).

### [L98] Lenat, The Dimensions of Context-Space

- **Document.** "The Dimensions of Context-Space", Doug Lenat, October 28, 1998, ©1998 Cycorp (p.1). Contents: 1 Summary; 2 Thinking about contexts (2.1-2.7); 3 Dimension-by-dimension discussion (12 dimensions); 4 Living in this 12-dimensional world; 5 Conclusion; References; Appendix A recap; Appendix B "An assertion only relies on some of the assumptions" (p.2).
- **Why Cyc adopted contexts, and what the first version was (pp.1, 3).** Benefits of being in a context: "Enabling us to ignore 99.999% of our knowledge so we can focus on 'the task at hand'"; "to be terse and sloppy in our communications"; "to accommodate apparently contradictory information, by partitioning it out to different contexts" (p.3). "During the 1984-1989 time period, as the Cyc common sense knowledge base [Lenat&Guha 90] grew ever larger, it became increasingly difficult to shoehorn every fact and rule into the same flat 'world.' Finally, in 1989, as Cyc exceeded 100,000 'rules' in size, we found it necessary to introduce an explicit context mechanism. That is, we divided the KB up into a lattice of hundreds of contexts" (p.3; footnote: "More precisely a DAG"). Early contexts were "full-fledged first-class reified (named) objects", with "a simple theory of 'lifting'", and two parts: "assumptions: a set of conjuncts to the antecedants of all the rules in that context" and "content: the set of rules/assertions said to hold, or be true in, that context"; "That late-1980's body of work culminated in a PhD thesis for R. V. Guha, the person who primarily implemented the Cyc context mechanism [Guha 91]" (p.3).
- **Lenat's verdict on the 1989-91 mechanism (pp.1, 4).** "That 1989-91 work turned out to be inadequate: it was too expensive to do nontrivial lifting (importing); to explicate the assumptions of each context; and to place each assertion/query into the proper context" (p.1). In detail: "The cost of lifting was computationally very high, so only trivial special cases of it were ever permitted"; "even when reified contexts were identified there was little short-term payoff in painstakingly explicating their assumptions. Not surprisingly, then, very little such assumption-articulating work was ever done"; package-style failures: P⇒Q asserted in C1 and P in C2, and Q not concluded in an asking context C3 "because one or both of the assertions were not importable"; and "the difficulty of deciding in what context an assertion belonged, or deciding from what point of view (context) an ASK was being made" (p.4).
- **The replacement (pp.1, 4).** "There are a dozen mostly-independent dimensions along which contexts vary (Absolute Time, Type of Time, Absolute Place, Type of Place, Culture, Sophistication/Security, Granularity, Epistemology, Argument-Preference, Topic, Justification, and Anthropacity.) Conversely, each region of that 12-dimensional space implicitly defines a context. In effect that space is the space of assumptions, and each assertion can be thought to hold true in some region of that space" (p.4). Content also has finer structure, "such as whether the assertion holds somewhere in (versus everywhere in) that context" (p.4). "A much larger calculus of contexts is required ... but it should be worth the cost: it should enable a much more efficient, much more focused sort of lifting ... and it should make it easier to place a rule or fact (or question) in the proper context" (p.4); the abstract calls it "virtual lifting" (p.1).
- **Shared context in communication (2.1, pp.5-8).** "Communication involves encoding and decoding, each of which are done in some context" (Fig. 1, p.5). "If you pluck an isolated line of code from a computer program, or an isolated sentence from a book, or an isolated cell from a spreadsheet, it will likely lose some or all of its meaning" (p.5). "Fred told the waiter he wanted some chips" licenses about twenty unstated inferences (p.6). Seven elements of shared context; the paper focuses on (3) shared real-world knowledge and (4) common-sense rules of thumb (pp.7-8). "That many of these are simplifications or misconceptions is irrelevant; what matters is the universality of the agreement, not the truth or accuracy of the information. After all, there can be, and are, other contexts that hold that more precise but less widely known model of that bit of the world" (p.8).
- **Assumptions as unstated antecedents (2.2, pp.12-13).** For an assertion P such as P999 "If it's raining outside, carry an umbrella", "applicability circumstances" are "a set of tests one could conjoin onto the antecedent of P" (p.12; fn. 5: with no antecedent they would form one, τ ⇒ P). Some tests are explicit; others "implicit but are so essential/common/fundamental/... that they are used unconsciously. These tests may be revealed by introspection, by having the system get the wrong parses to sentences, or by getting the wrong answers to questions posed to it ... At any given moment, one has the illusion of having found them all. This is akin to programmers believing they've found the last bug in their code" (p.12). Fig. 2f lists 25 implicit assumptions of P999 (performer is human, sane, not a baby, not dead; time after the umbrella's invention; culture uses umbrellas for rain; not ammonia rain on Venus; etc.) (p.13). Writing these once as a context's assumptions "greatly simplifies and speeds up the process of KE-ing"; "In some cases, you won't know what exactly the assumptions are ... but you may still know that some set of assertions all make more or less the same assumptions. You could then clump those assertions together into a context, and provide at least an informal comment explaining the sense of what the context is meant to capture ... Most computer programs that have ever been written fall into this category; the modules of the computer program share a set of never-explicated assumptions in common with each other and, hopefully, in common with the user of the application program" (pp.12-13).
- **Nine years of microtheories in practice (2.2, p.14).** "For the past nine years, we've been carving the Cyc KB up into a set of 'contexts' or 'microtheories.'" Gains: assertions in a specialized context are "more terse and simpler in form", and similar assertions are close at hand to copy and edit. But "is this speedup in the rate of ontologizing sufficient? - the answer is No ... It is distracting and time-consuming for the knowledge enterer to select (or, even worse, create) precisely the right context each time ... And it can't be skipped: doing a slipshod job of placing each new assertion just leads to bugs (wrong answers, failure to apply relevant knowledge, etc.) ... where a 'wrong answer' turns out to be due to someone simply having placed an assertion into the wrong context. Or, just as bad, the knowledge enterer ignores the context mechanism and the result is a small number of enormous contexts." "So the challenge is to find a good intermediate-granularity carving of the KB into contexts: too coarse-grained is like having no contexts at all ... too fine-grained makes it hard to find the right context for a new piece of knowledge" (p.14).
- **Contexts and inference (2.3, pp.15-16).** Contexts are a third inference-guidance mechanism besides heuristic-level modules and search heuristics: "carving it up into meaningful subsets such that intra-subset deductions are more likely to be productive than cross-subset deductions. Each of those subsets is a context" (p.15). Too coarse: irrelevant assertions together, and "many of the assumptions will only actually be relied on by a small fraction of the assertions"; too fine: "the high cost of lots of liftings" (p.16).
- **Lifting and the granularity rule (2.4, p.16).** Missing assumptions surface as wrong answers: "the missing assumption will be painfully clear, and can then be formalized and added as an explicit assumption. This is a form of knowledge debugging, in many ways analogous to program debugging. It is a correctness issue, not just an efficiency issue" (p.16). "When we lift an axiom P from context C1 into context C2, and use it there in some inference (with, say, axioms Q, R,... from C2), we need to ensure that there is no logical inconsistency between the C1 assumptions and the C2 assumptions. If assertions only depend on a few of their context's assumptions, this will prevent a lot of otherwise-useful and valid lifting from taking place. So: most (if not all) of the assertions in a context should actually rely on most (if not all) of the domainAssumptions of that context." If an assumption α "only pertains to a sliver of C1's assertions": "Split off that sliver as its own little context C1′ (and make α one of C1′'s assumptions)" or "just conjoin α to the antecedent of each of those assertions and leave them in C1" (p.16). Lifting P109 from C1 to C2 means asserting "some P109′ whose meaning there (in context C2) is more or less the same as the meaning of P109 in context C1" (p.16).
- **Lifting by conjoining the source's assumptions (pp.17-19).** Fig. 2h: C1 with assumptions A, B lifts P109 into C2 (assumptions B) as A′ ⇒ P109′ (p.17). "Sometimes we can just go ahead and assert P109, over into context C2, with no changes ... But often it turns out that some of C1's assumptions - on which P109 depends - are not made in C2, and it would be better to conjoin them on the antecedent of P109"; Heaven's Gate example: a New York Times editorial would say "If the Heaven's Gate cult's beliefs are right, then the comet Hale-Bopp has a UFO hiding behind it" (p.17). Fn. 8: A′ ⇒ P109′ is primed because "some other aspects of P109, and even of A, might be slightly changed in C2 compared to C1. E.g., the arity of a predicate might be different, or the spelling of a term" (p.17). "We can never fully explicate all assumptions underlying the knowledge we express ... Contexts, just like the real-world situations they describe, are 'rich objects;' i.e., there is an infinity of things one could say about a context, and of course only a small finite number of those will ever be explicated" (pp.17-18). Importing into a more specific context: "all the domain assumptions are (by default) still satisfied in the more specialized context, so any assertion can just be imported (unchanged) ... This is still just a default"; "There should be precious little in the BaseKB, since all of it must be freely importable - unchanged - to every other context" (p.18). Implication-shaped assumptions (MilInfantryCombatMt assumes persons are male) rewrite conjuncts when lifted: "almost everyone over 30 has a wife" becomes "almost every man over 30", "almost every infantry soldier over 30", or "almost every male infantry soldier over 30" depending on the target (p.18). "In cases where it's not so clear how exactly to insert the old context's assumption into the assertion being lifted, one can always fall back on the crude but logically equivalent approach of conjoining, to the antecedent of the assertion, all the assumptions of the old context it's being lifted from" (p.19).
- **Assertions have domains of applicability (2.5, pp.19-20).** Dual view: "assertions are the real objects, and they happen to have (i.e. hold true in) some contexts"; "Pigs can talk" can be asserted with the contexts where it holds (Animal Farm, Babe, Sesame Street), made "more parsimonious by finding and asserting the most general mutually-incommensurable contexts in which P held" (p.19). One context per assertion is inefficient (finding the right context, constant lifting) (p.19), but "when you decide to enter some new assertion P into the KB, you should think about, and articulate to Cyc, the (most general) contexts in which P holds"; fn. 11: "how generally P can/should be stated, how likely P is to be true ... what new vocabulary should/must be introduced in order to efficiently state P to Cyc, whether the English paraphrase of P is okay" (p.20). Questions too: with an unspecified context the answer "could/should really be a set of <context, answer> pairs" (p.20).
- **Contexts as regions (2.6, p.20).** "Point 1: Besides thinking of a context as 'a named node in an ontology', let's also think of it as being 'a region in some n-dimensional space'" (fn. 12: the named-node view is "A DAG ... of reified context 'nodes' related by generalization/specialization 'links'"; fn. 13: a context is "mostly specified, it is nearly unique, once you assign those coordinates. That's why the title of this section says 'approximated by'") (p.20).
- **genlMt was too vague (p.21).** "The old Cyc genlMt predicate is just one simple relationship that can hold among two contexts; it roughly corresponds to 'proper subset-of (or similar restriction) in some m dimensions, and no change (that is, equality) in all the other n-m dimensions.'" It meant "is a temporal slice of" (The1920sUSA, TheTwentiethCenturyUSA), "has a more restricted set of default actors than" (Human/AnimalActivities), or "is a more precise and accurate theory than" (NewtonianPhysicsMt, NaivePhysicsMt); "our naiveté in the early 1990's in having just this one vague genlMt predicate is akin to the error that other ontologists make when they have just one a-kind-of relation" (p.21). Analogies (chemical-formula space; quarks; Paul Cohen's "slot-space" used at MCC to guess the composition of two predicates, e.g. owns ∘ physical-parts = owns) (pp.21-22).
- **Choosing dimensions (p.23).** Utility of a dimension "should be measured by: success in separating out mutually-irrelevant portions of knowledge from each other; ease of computing the overlap / disjointness of subsections of the dimension; how much they tease apart notorious large KB clumps (e.g., HumanActivitiesMt); whether the points and regions (especially the extreme ones) correspond to semantically familiar and important real-world concepts and considerations"; and "should not be measured by: how closely it satisfies some arbitrary philosophical/linguistic/... model; whether there happens to be an existing English word succinctly capturing it; notions of 'cleverness', 'cleanness', 'novelty', 'symmetry', 'academic interest'" (p.23). "Although the general domain assumptions/ relevance/lifting problem is arbitrarily hard, we are going to use an 80/20 approach"; the dozen came from "enumerating a much larger set of dimensions (over 100), and then clumping together the two most closely related, again and again" (p.23). Dimension order matters like quantifier order (earlierDimension) but "will rarely matter" (p.23).
- **The 12 dimensions (2.7, p.24).** Absolute Time; Type Of Time; Absolute Place; Type Of Place; Culture; Sophistication/Security; Topic/Usage; Granularity ("phenomena and details which are (and are not) ignored"); Modality/Disposition/Epistemology; Argument-Preference ("local rules for how to resolve pro-con argument disputes"); Justification ("are things in this context generally proven, observed, on faith..."); Let's ("local bindings of variables etc.") (p.24). "Point 2: Placing each new assertion P into the ideal context is expensive today. Idea to speed it up: have it be mostly just stating/choosing 12 meta-level values." "Point 3: Automated inference over a million-rule KB is expensive today. Idea to speed it up: localize the search, favoring the content in same/nearby contexts" (p.24).
- **Non-dimensions and the leftover assumptions (pp.25-26).** Not dimensions: tags for probability/certainty/monotonicity, forward/backward use, whether propositions hold throughout an interval or at some points, interval openness, quoting, physical attributes such as temperature and pressure, and "anthropacity" ("what does it presume that there 'is' in the world"), which "can largely be computed from the various assertions (and assumptions) of the context" (p.25). "Specifying values for the most important dozen dimensions doesn't always fully specify the context ... This is analogous to a hashing function"; each 12-space region is "a 'bucket' of contexts" organized by genlMt and other relations (pp.25-26). "some of the domain assumptions of a context will now be specified by its 12-space location, and the leftover domain assumptions will be specified explicitly (when they are known/thought about) as Cyc formulas which are then the listed domainAssumptions of that context" (p.26).
- **(Section 3, the dimension-by-dimension discussion, printed pp.27-64, was not read except 3.8 Granularity, pp.54-55.)**
- **Granularity (3.8, pp.54-55).** Integrative levels (molecular to society): "assertions at one level do not simply translate up or down to the next level. Each level is almost its own world" (p.54). Sub-dimensions: "What sized" objects and events are relevant; "What specificity" of classes and relationships; these ask "What distinctions matter?"; another asks "What phenomena matter? For example, does friction matter, do relativistic effects matter, does weather matter"; and "Level of formal entity" (a book versus an edition versus its content; a chess game as abstract moves or a physical event) (pp.54-55). Example: in #$UnitedStatesGeographyMt the contiguous US is #$spatiallyContinuous although offshore islands are not; "the spatial granularity of the #$UnitedStatesGeographyMt context is larger (coarser) than any context in which those sandbars, islands, etc. are known about. In that context, they don't even exist ... That could be stated as a predicated domain-assumption of that context, but having the Granularity dimension enables us to state it succinctly as a Granularity value" (p.55). "The Granularity dimension is where we will integrate 'order of magnitude' reasoning into Cyc", via predicates such as (#$mtLinearDistanceValue C INTERVAL): objects whose longest axis falls in INTERVAL "should be attended to ... and those which are much larger or smaller shouldn't", e.g. #$SleepMt 1 mm to 10 m (p.55).
- **Knowledge entering in the new scheme (4.1, pp.65-69).** KE-ing is "a two-step process: 1. Specify a context - that is, indicate the region of 12-space you're talking about. 2. Tell or ask Cyc something 'in' that context" (p.65). Five ways to specify a context: copy the 12-space settings of a known assertion's context; combine settings of several ("conjoin, or disjoin, or intersect, or union"); adjust settings; specify from scratch (12 "specifyings"); or name a reified context (pp.66-67). "Point 8: ... given an assertion, return the contexts (the sets of 12 settings) in which it holds"; "Point 9: ... given a particular context, modify one (or more) of those 12 dimension settings a little" (p.66). Settings constrain each other, and "even just the appearance of various terms in P can provide a good starting guess for the 12 settings" (p.67). "The main discernable difference should be that sometimes some of the antecedent's clauses can be replaced by a change in one (or more) of the 12 dimensions" (p.68). The scheme must avoid "'backsliding' (to the all-in-BaseKB laziness) that has proven so harmful"; each dimension poses questions to the knowledge enterer, e.g. "What's the smallest physical distance worth distinguishing, from this assertion's point of view? What is the applicable time period?" (pp.68-69).
- **Inference in the new scheme (4.2, pp.69-70).** "think of contexts as determining not an absolute relevant/irrelevant black&white border, but rather as determining a partial ordering of relevance" (p.69). Each dimension gets its own generalization predicates, e.g. (finer C1 C2), defined as the conjunction of six specialized "no coarser than" predicates (mtRelevantPhenomena, mtSubsetAbstractionLevel, etc.) (p.69). New questions: "in which contexts is P true? in which contexts is P false? (conversely: in which could P possibly hold?)", and "in which contexts is term Z even present?"; "what we want, clearly, is the maximal range over which it holds" (pp.69-70). Finding a baby on the doorstep moves one into a larger context; better to think of "a set of contexts that are 'activated.' A small number of lifted assertions then connects the two contexts, but most of the reasoning that goes on is still in just one narrow context" (Law & Order example) (p.70).
- **Conclusion (5, pp.71-72).** For inference: search limited to one or a few contexts "plus a very small number of imported assertions"; "Inconsistent - not just irrelevant - information is kept isolated in other contexts"; shared assumptions "factored out" (p.71). "Contexts don't do the whole job, of course, in speeding up inference" (p.71). For KE: "Even if the assumptions of a context are only much later - or even never - fully known and specified, it may be relatively easy and natural to tell that assertion P belongs in the same context as assertion Q"; the dimensions let one "easily 'write' some conjuncts on P's antecedent just by sliding/dialing" (p.71). A Cyc context has 8 parts: "(1) content: a group of generally-consistent assertions, sharing many assumptions (2) core assumptions: ... nontrivially assumed by almost all the content assertions (3) localized assumptions: ... relied on only by some specific assertions in the content ... stated in the form of a class of exceptions to an existing rule, thanks to Minimization. (4) term or nat ... (5) inter-context links ... (6) lifting rules: one class of (5), which 'map' assertions from one context to another (7) n-dimensional location ... (8) assertions that derive some of (2),(5),(6) from that n-dimensional location" (p.72). "Point 10: Moving to n-dimensional context-space will likely change KE-ing back from 'prepare a big KE file, statically' to 'tell Cyc things, one at a time, and let Cyc help you by commenting/reacting to each thing you tell it.'" (p.72). Cyc should do "a decent job at semi-automatically positioning assertions among the n dimensions"; partial rules can help, e.g. using an automobile's date of invention "to automatically constrain the relevant time of assertion P" (p.73). The paper gives no measured results for the 12-dimensional scheme; it is stated as a design and expectation ("we expect this rich new context scheme will catalyze and revolutionize both KE-ing and inferencing", p.73).
- **References (p.74):** Buvač 1996, Guha 1991, Lenat & Guha 1990, Lenat 1995, McCarthy & Hayes 1969, McCarthy 1993, Miller 1978, Murdock et al. 1987, NLM UMLS 1996, Peterson 1994 (Art & Architecture Thesaurus). No algebraic-specification, theory-interpretation or Little Theories work.
- **Appendix A recap (p.75).** Contexts of "a manageable number (e.g., 1000) of assertions"; knowledge entering in three stages, "(i) find the relevant context, (ii) find a similar assertion in it, (iii) copy it and alter that copy" (fn. 37: "a KB of size n would have √n contexts each with about √n assertions"); "Placing each new assertion P into the ideal context is very expensive today - it may take a person longer to situate an assertion than it took them to mentally compose it in the first place!" (p.75).
- **Appendix B, an assertion relies on only some assumptions (pp.77-78).** "our characterization of a context as a bundle of assertions with 'a set of shared assumptions' is a bit too simplistic": R1 (wipe your feet) assumes feet, R2 (carry an umbrella) assumes arms; listing both as context assumptions "feels wrong"; rather than pushing the conditions into the axioms (Fig. B2, "heavy-handed"), state exception rules E1, E2 ("If a person has no (use of their) feet, then they are an exception to R1") (p.77). "The exception axioms (E1 and E2) could and should be lifted from one context to another, just like any other axioms"; their own assumptions (no magic) yield "second-order exception rules capable of blocking E1 and E2", e.g. in the Sorcerer's Apprentice context (p.78). The scheme rests on minimization: "Given a thing, and a way it could be abnormal, it probably isn't abnormal that way" (Fig. B4), via (minimizeAround P) (p.78). Two sorts of assumption: "Type-(a): The core shared assumptions of a context; those which practically all the assertions in that context really do depend on"; "Type-(b): A set of mutually non-contradictory 'special-case' assumptions that some of the assertions in the context really do depend on"; Type-(a) are asserted about the context as a whole, Type-(b) as separate exception rules (p.78).

### Substance: My comparison

The two ideas solve the same engineering problem from opposite ends, and several mechanisms correspond closely. Points of correspondence:

1. **Small theory with its own vocabulary.** [LT]: "different portions of mathematics are developed in various different formal axiomatic theories", each result stated in the theory with the least structure it needs (institutions report, [LT] pp.2, 4). [G91]: a context structure gives each context "A Language L(C)" and "A set of structures CM(C)" (p.21); a microtheory is "a theory of some topic" whose different "assumptions and simplifications" are recorded (p.41). [L98]: a context has content plus "core assumptions ... nontrivially assumed by almost all the content assertions" (p.72). As objects these are the same kind of thing: a signature plus a theory (or model class).
2. **Importing a small theory into a bigger setting.** [MB98]'s "importation of above-theory" into blocks (eqs. 4-6, p.8): bridge axioms map on(x,y) and above(x,y) to on(x,y,s), above(x,y,s) through spec-sit(s), and one axiom transports every fact of above-theory into every situation context, so Theorem (above) holds in blocks. Its stated motive is reuse: "imagine that above-theory was much larger" (p.8). This is in effect a theory interpretation of above-theory into blocks, parametrized by s, used to transport theorems, which is the use [LT] gives interpretations (institutions report, [LT] pp.2-3). [G91]'s compositional lifting does the same at the level of symbols: lifting rules "specify how these vocabularies can be mapped between each other", and formulas are lifted compositionally from the symbol map (p.33; CR, p.34), which is how a syntactic translation of a theory interpretation is built.
3. **Assumptions become relativization.** [G91]: when the origin context assumes P of all its objects and the target does not, ∀x R(x) lifts to ∀x P(x) → R1(x) (p.83), and scope is the denotation of "∀" (p.82); [L98]: P109 lifts as A′ ⇒ P109′, or crudely by conjoining all the source's assumptions to the antecedent (pp.17, 19). [LT] says an interpretation tells one "how to select a subdomain and distinguished values that furnish a model of T" (institutions report, [LT] p.3), i.e. it relativizes quantifiers to a subdomain. The same move: the source's background assumptions turn into a guard in the target.
4. **Minimality.** [LT]'s rule is to prove each result in the theory with "minimal axiomatizations ... This ensures maximal generality of reuse" (institutions report, [LT] p.4). [L98]'s rule is that "most (if not all) of the assertions in a context should actually rely on most (if not all) of the domainAssumptions of that context", else split the sliver into "its own little context" (p.16), because an assumption that an assertion does not use blocks valid lifting. Both say: do not let a theory carry assumptions its results do not need, because those assumptions block reuse. Different motive words ("generality of reuse", "valid lifting"), same rule.
5. **Local, explicit assumptions.** [LT]'s context interpretations hold under local assumptions "so long as our position in a proof licenses us to use the assumptions" (institutions report, [LT] pp.12-13). McCarthy's entering a context is "analogous to assuming c in a system of natural deduction" ([M93] p.5), and assuming(c,p) with importation and discharge rules is exactly natural-deduction assumption handling ([MB98] p.10).

Where they differ:

6. **What carries a result across, and with what guarantee.** An [LT] interpretation is checked: its obligations (translated source axioms) must be target theorems, and then every source theorem is a target theorem (institutions report, [LT] pp.2-3, 7). Lifting rules are axioms that someone asserts, mostly as defaults with abnormality conditions ([M93] p.4; [G91] DCR pp.33-34, "We cheat and use defaults", p.40); nothing in the read works makes a lifting rule discharge an obligation, and [G91] states soundness of its inference rules but says "I don't know whether the logic is complete" (p.26) and gives the semantics of lifting via a correspondence function that "we simply don't have" (p.39). So theory interpretations give theorem transport with a proof obligation; lifting gives transport by fiat, defeasibly, with the bridge's correctness left to whoever wrote it. [GG01] is the exception in the read context work: its bridge rules are judged against a semantic compatibility relation, and for the magic-box system the calculus is proved sound and complete with respect to the intended class of models (Theorems A.1, A.2, pp.247-249). That is a correctness criterion for bridges, but relative to a compatibility relation someone chose, and monotone (no defaults).
7. **Consistency.** [LT] argues for "a single familiar logic" (institutions report, [LT] p.5) and uses interpretations to establish relative consistency of a theory (p.13); nothing recorded from [LT] tolerates an inconsistent collection of theories. Microtheories exist so that the KB can be globally inconsistent: "We should only be required to maintain local consistency within each contexts" ([G91] p.10; p.42), and conflicting adequate theories (particle and wave, p.45) are kept apart.
8. **Truth versus adequacy.** [LT] transports truths. [G91] replaces truth by adequacy: "We need to replace the concept of a theory being true with the concept of a theory being adequate for the problem we are trying to solve" (p.44); importing is blocked when a theory's error exceeds the problem's required precision (A68, p.94), and among adequate theories "the system prefers the cheapest adequate theory" (p.95). Nothing like this is in the [LT] statements recorded in the institutions report.
9. **Direction of the motive.** [LT] starts from formal theories and builds a network to reuse proofs. Contexts start from a messy KB (Cyc at 100,000 rules, [L98] p.3) and an informal world, and treat a context as "a rich object" that "cannot be completely described" ([G91] p.8; [M93] p.2; [L98] pp.17-18). [LT]'s theories are fully given.
10. **Model side.** Neither line, as read, states a satisfaction condition linking a symbol map to a model map. [G91]'s correspondence function CF between relation tuples of models, restricted to the shared domain (p.38), is the nearest object to the model part β of an institution morphism in the institutions report; it is defined only informally, and partial.

11. **Translation versus relation.** A theory interpretation (and an institution comorphism) is a map from one vocabulary into another. [GG01]'s compatibility relation is not a map: it is a set of sequences of sets of local models, one per context, saying which local views can coexist (pp.227, 229), and bridge rules may go both ways (br_12, br_21, p.243). It is closer to the information-flow report's channel, whose core tokens are "the specific connection" between tokens on each side, than to an interpretation. [GG01] motivates this by the absence of a global description: projecting views from an axiomatized world "is based on the hypothesis that we have a complete description of the world ... This is not always the case" (p.224); a single global language "does not seem to work in those cases where there is no global scheme describing the system" (p.246). Little Theories, as recorded, always has a target theory to interpret into.

**Direct answer.** In lineage, no: the read works do not cite each other and share no authors. In substance, yes, closely: a microtheory with lifting axioms is a small theory with interpretation-like bridges, and McCarthy & Buvač's importation of above-theory is a theory interpretation in all but name. The difference is the guarantee: Little Theories checks its bridges and transports theorems soundly within one logic; microtheories assert their bridges as defeasible defaults, tolerate global inconsistency, and judge theories by adequacy for a task rather than truth. The formal branch of context work ([GG01]) closes part of that gap with bridge rules proved sound and complete against a compatibility relation among local models, which relates views rather than mapping one theory into another. (My comparison.)

## Synthesis

Is contextual work useful for building a bridge from a fuzzy domain to a formalism, and for choosing the closest formalism? Restricted to the read works; lines that combine sources or relate them to other reports are marked "(my combination)".

1. **It is the only read literature that treats the fuzzy side as the normal case.** Contexts exist because representations depend on unstated assumptions that "cannot be completely described" ([G91] p.8; [M93] p.2; [L98] pp.17-18), because vocabulary choice silently excludes phenomena ([G91] p.5), and because "Most computer programs that have ever been written ... share a set of never-explicated assumptions" ([L98] pp.12-13). The institutions and information-flow reports found the first step from the informal world outside their theories; the bridges report covers it with practices (designations, K, explicit imprecision) rather than a formalism (my combination). Context work does not make that step formal either, but it gives it a container: write the formal content inside a named context and keep the unstated part attached to the context, to be made explicit "asynchronously, later on demand" ([G91] p.12).

2. **Context assumptions are the K of S, K ⊢ R, made first-class and per-theory.** Zave & Jackson's domain knowledge K is validated informally and the proof's claim about the world is conditional on it (bridges report). In [G91] and [L98] those assumptions are attached to each context, can be partial, and are surfaced the way K failures are: "the missing assumption will be painfully clear ... a form of knowledge debugging ... a correctness issue" ([L98] p.16); a car program states its assumptions "so the system knows when it is dealing with a situation beyond its sphere of competence ... not giving any answer is certainly better than giving incorrect answers" ([G91] p.81). The engine-price example is the same pattern: the databases' hidden assumptions about spares and warranty "will probably not be contained in the Navy data base ... Rather, this information is kept in some manual", and must be written into the problem-solving context before the two can be used together ([MB98] p.15). (My combination.) What the context work adds over the bridges report: assumptions as an explicit antecedent when a fact leaves its home context (∀x P(x) → R1(x), [G91] p.83; A′ ⇒ P109′, [L98] p.17), so the K-conditionality of a result travels with it instead of being lost.

3. **Scope and abnormality correspond to channel theory's normal tokens.** [G91] distinguishes objects outside a theory's scope ("He is simply beyond the scope of the theory", p.7; ¬presentIn, pp.82-84) from exceptions to defaults (ab predicates, p.85), and [L98] splits core assumptions from localized ones handled as exception rules under minimization (p.78). The information-flow report's local logics bind constraints only on normal tokens, with abnormal tokens "classified but flagged as strange" (MSR p.337 there). Scope ≈ the normal-token set; ab-literals ≈ per-constraint abnormality. (My combination; no read work relates them.)

4. **Lifting is a translation without the satisfaction-condition guarantee.** The institutions report's lesson is that a bridge is correct when satisfaction agrees on both sides, and confirmations come back only under model expansion. [G91] defines lifting semantically through a correspondence function between models, restricted to the shared domain (p.38), which plays the role of the model map, but has no way to obtain it (p.39) and in practice writes lifting as default biconditionals that are "inadequate" for costatement (p.40). So, read through the institutions report: a lifting rule is an unverified comorphism, and conclusions moved by it carry no proof-level guarantee (my combination). [MB98]'s above-theory importation is the one read case where the bridge is a plain translation plus a transport axiom, i.e. where an interpretation-style check could be done. [GG01] supplies the missing semantics for one family of context systems: contexts are sets of local models, a compatibility relation says which local models may coexist, consequence across contexts is defined by pruning sequences (Def 3.6, p.231), and bridge rules are proved sound and complete for the magic-box case (pp.247-249). Read through the institutions and information-flow reports, a compatibility relation plays the role of a channel core relating tokens of different views rather than a comorphism mapping one logic into another (my combination). Two consequences [GG01] states that matter for bug-finding bridges: local consequence always carries over into the multi-context system (Theorem 3.1, p.232), but the converse fails because of assumptions in other contexts (p.233); and a context may be a set of models, so a sentence can be neither true nor false in it (p.229), which is how a partial observation of a system can be represented without inventing the unobserved part.

5. **Choosing the closest formalism: the read works give an adequacy criterion, not a correctness one.** [G91] states the problem directly: "the system must determine which single theory or which combination of theories to use to solve a given problem ... often called the 'model formation' problem" (p.45), and the create, lift, enter, solve, exit sequence exists "to bring the choice of the theory used to solve a problem within the scope of the problem solving" (p.10). Its criteria:
   - adequacy for the task replaces truth (p.44; p.93);
   - a theory's predictions are imported only if its error estimate is within the precision the problem needs (A68, p.94), or, when error cannot be estimated, if the theory's assumptions are known to be reasonable for the case (Newtonian mechanics below half light speed; mileage data below 10,000 ft) (pp.95-96);
   - among adequate theories, "the system prefers the cheapest adequate theory" (p.95);
   - "Assumptions ... and errors in the predictions made by theories are two sides of the same coin. The errors are the cost of the assumptions that a theory makes" (p.95).
   [G91]'s "shift and solve" (switch into an existing context, run a conventional solver there, lift the answer back, p.49) is the read work's version of borrowing a formalism's tool (my combination with the institutions report). Combined with the institutions report's verdict classes, a candidate "closest formalism" would be: the cheapest target whose assumptions hold for the case (or whose error is within tolerance) and whose bridge carries back the verdict you need (my combination; no read work states this rule).

6. **Granularity is the part of a bridge that chooses what to ignore.** [L98]'s Granularity dimension asks "What distinctions matter?" and "What phenomena matter? For example, does friction matter, do relativistic effects matter" (p.54), and states a context's attended size range as data (p.55). Smith's "models have to be partial" (bridges report) and the information-flow report's schemes of individuation are the same question (my combination). Unknown from the read pages: how Cyc uses these values in lifting; Section 3 was mostly not read.

7. **Practical warnings from the one large deployment.** Cyc's 1989-91 mechanism "turned out to be inadequate: it was too expensive to do nontrivial lifting (importing); to explicate the assumptions of each context; and to place each assertion/query into the proper context" ([L98] p.1); "only trivial special cases of it were ever permitted"; "very little such assumption-articulating work was ever done" (p.4); placing assertions wrongly "just leads to bugs ... a 'wrong answer' turns out to be due to someone simply having placed an assertion into the wrong context" (p.14). [G91] itself notes lifting to completion is "infeasible" (p.50) and that writing the rules is "the bulk of the effort" (p.41). The read works offer granularity advice (not too coarse, not too fine, [L98] pp.14, 16; √n contexts of √n assertions, p.75) and the 12-dimension scheme, for which [L98] gives no measured results (p.73). For a bug-finding bridge this argues for few, explicit assumptions per small theory and checked translations rather than many default lifting rules (my combination).

8. **Partial views without a world model.** [GG01]'s magic box is the read works' clearest picture of the fuzzy-domain situation: two observers with different vocabularies, neither seeing the whole, and situations that both views cannot distinguish (Fig. 4, p.224). Its answer is not to axiomatize the world and project views but to state only how the views constrain each other (constraints (5)-(7), p.235). For a bug-finding bridge where the formal side is one view (a spec) and the program's observable behaviour another, this suggests stating compatibility constraints between the two views rather than assuming a complete model of the system (my combination; no read work applies it to programs).

9. **Gaps (unknown from the read works):** a soundness or completeness result linking Cyc-style default lifting rules to [G91]'s model-level account (the [GG01] results cover monotone bridge rules only); any procedure for discovering a context's assumptions other than wrong answers and introspection; any evaluation of the 12-dimensional scheme; whether Buvač's logics (local, unread) or Bozzato et al. 2018 on justifiable exceptions (local, unread) give defeasible lifting a semantic criterion.
